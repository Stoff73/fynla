Portfolio diversification is measured by assessing asset correlation, concentration risk, and quantitative metrics like the Diversification Ratio and the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI). These tools help quantify how effectively a portfolio reduces risk by spreading investments across different assets and sectors. 
Key Metrics for Measuring Diversification
Correlation Coefficient: This statistical measure ranges from -1.0 to +1.0 and indicates how two assets move in relation to each other. Assets with low or negative correlations (closer to -1.0 or 0) offer the greatest diversification benefits, as their price movements tend to offset each other during market fluctuations.
Diversification Ratio: A formal metric that quantifies risk reduction. It is calculated by dividing the weighted average of individual asset volatilities by the actual portfolio volatility. A higher ratio indicates greater diversification benefits and lower overall portfolio risk, while an ideal, fully diversified portfolio would have a ratio closer to zero (for some definitions, or 1 for others, depending on the specific formula used).
Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI): Originally an economic measure of market concentration, HHI can be adapted to portfolios by summing the squares of each asset's weight (proportion). A lower HHI (or a higher value for its complement, 1-HHI) indicates a more diversified portfolio, with values ranging from 0 (highly diversified) to 1 (a single-asset portfolio).
Effective Number of Bets (ENB): This measure, related to the diversification ratio, estimates the true number of independent risk factors or "uncorrelated bets" within a portfolio, considering correlations between assets. A higher ENB suggests better diversification.
Sharpe Ratio: While primarily a performance measure, the Sharpe ratio evaluates risk-adjusted return using the portfolio's standard deviation (total risk). It implicitly measures diversification because a well-diversified portfolio should have a lower standard deviation for the same level of return, resulting in a higher Sharpe ratio. 
Practical Approaches
For most investors, a simple, practical approach is to adhere to general guidelines and use common sense. 
Asset Allocation: Ensure investments are spread across different asset classes such as stocks, bonds, and real estate.
Sector & Geographic Diversification: Avoid overconcentration in a single industry or country to mitigate specific risks.
Position Weight Limits: A common rule of thumb is the "5% rule," suggesting no single stock should comprise more than 5% of the total portfolio value to prevent outsize losses from one position.
Regular Monitoring: Periodically monitor and rebalance the portfolio to maintain desired diversification levels and adjust for changing market conditions and shifting asset correlations.
