**Business Structures, Taxes, and Key Obligations for Running a Business
in the UK**

**Business Structure Options in the UK**

When starting a business in the UK, you must choose a legal structure.
Your choice affects your **legal liability**, **taxation**, and
**administrative
duties**[[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/set-up-business#:~:text=The%20business%20structure%20you%20choose,tax%20and%20your%20legal%20responsibilities)[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/set-up-business#:~:text=Sole%20trader%20Limited%20company%20Legal,Directors%20need%20to%20follow)]{.underline}.
The main business formats are outlined below:

- **Sole Trader:** A simple structure where the business is owned and
  run by one individual. Sole traders are **self-employed** and can
  start trading immediately (though must register with HMRC once
  earnings exceed £1,000 in a tax
  year)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/set-up-business#:~:text=Registering%20You%20can%20start%20trading,for%20example).
  You keep all profits after tax, but you have **unlimited liability**
  -- meaning you are personally responsible for all business
  debts[[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader#:~:text=Legal%20risks%20or%20%E2%80%98liability%E2%80%99)[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader#:~:text=You%20can%20start%20trading%20straight,can%20choose%20to%20register%20earlier)]{.underline}.
  There's minimal paperwork: you need to **keep records** of
  income/expenses and file an annual Self Assessment tax
  return[[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader#:~:text=Keeping%20records%20and%20accounts)[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader#:~:text=When%20you%20submit%20your%20Self,36%C2%A0based%20on%20your%20profits)]{.underline}.
  A sole trader can employ staff if needed. This format is easy to set
  up and gives full control, but personal assets are at risk if the
  business incurs
  debts[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader#:~:text=Legal%20risks%20or%20%E2%80%98liability%E2%80%99).

- **Business Partnership:** This is similar to sole trading but with
  **two or more people** jointly running the business. Partners share
  decision-making and **split the profits**, and each pays tax on their
  share[[companieshouse.blog.gov.uk](https://companieshouse.blog.gov.uk/2018/07/18/choosing-the-right-business-structure/#:~:text=Partnership)[companieshouse.blog.gov.uk](https://companieshouse.blog.gov.uk/2018/07/18/choosing-the-right-business-structure/#:~:text=A%20partnership%20agreement%20document%20outlines,submit%20a%20separate%20tax%20return)]{.underline}.
  Like sole traders, partners generally have **unlimited liability** in
  an ordinary partnership -- each partner is personally liable for the
  business's
  debts[[companieshouse.blog.gov.uk]{.underline}](https://companieshouse.blog.gov.uk/2018/07/18/choosing-the-right-business-structure/#:~:text=In%20a%20standard%20partnership%20all,debts%20owed%20by%20the%20business).
  It's wise (though not legally required) to have a **partnership
  agreement** outlining each partner's rights and what happens if
  someone
  leaves[[companieshouse.blog.gov.uk]{.underline}](https://companieshouse.blog.gov.uk/2018/07/18/choosing-the-right-business-structure/#:~:text=The%20%E2%80%98nominated%20partner%E2%80%99%20is%20responsible,tax%20returns%20and%20record%20keeping).
  A **nominated partner** must register the partnership with HMRC and
  handle the partnership tax return, while all partners must register as
  self-employed and file individual tax
  returns[[companieshouse.blog.gov.uk](https://companieshouse.blog.gov.uk/2018/07/18/choosing-the-right-business-structure/#:~:text=When%20you%20set%20up%20a,business%20partnership%20you%20need%20to)[companieshouse.blog.gov.uk](https://companieshouse.blog.gov.uk/2018/07/18/choosing-the-right-business-structure/#:~:text=A%20partnership%20agreement%20document%20outlines,submit%20a%20separate%20tax%20return)]{.underline}.
  Partnerships are relatively easy to form and can pool partners' skills
  and capital, but all partners remain fully liable for any liabilities
  incurred by the
  business[[companieshouse.blog.gov.uk]{.underline}](https://companieshouse.blog.gov.uk/2018/07/18/choosing-the-right-business-structure/#:~:text=Between%20two%20or%20more%20individuals,who%20share%20management%20and%20profits).

- **Limited Liability Partnership (LLP):** An LLP is a hybrid structure
  that offers **limited liability** to all partners (called "members")
  while retaining the tax flexibility of a partnership. It must be
  incorporated at Companies House and have at least two designated
  members responsible for filing annual
  accounts[[companieshouse.blog.gov.uk]{.underline}](https://companieshouse.blog.gov.uk/2018/07/18/choosing-the-right-business-structure/#:~:text=Limited%20liability%20partnership%20).
  Members' personal assets are protected -- liability is limited to what
  they invested or guaranteed -- but for tax purposes, each member is
  taxed on their share of profits as in a normal partnership (each
  member registers as self-employed for Self
  Assessment)[[companieshouse.blog.gov.uk](https://companieshouse.blog.gov.uk/2018/07/18/choosing-the-right-business-structure/#:~:text=Just%20as%20with%20a%20limited,have%20given%20when%20raising%20loans)[companieshouse.blog.gov.uk](https://companieshouse.blog.gov.uk/2018/07/18/choosing-the-right-business-structure/#:~:text=Some%20or%20all%20partners%20have,elements%20of%20partnerships%20and%20corporations)]{.underline}.
  This structure is common for professional firms. In summary, an LLP
  provides the liability protection of a company with the organizational
  flexibility of a
  partnership[[companieshouse.blog.gov.uk](https://companieshouse.blog.gov.uk/2018/07/18/choosing-the-right-business-structure/#:~:text=Just%20as%20with%20a%20limited,have%20given%20when%20raising%20loans)[companieshouse.blog.gov.uk](https://companieshouse.blog.gov.uk/2018/07/18/choosing-the-right-business-structure/#:~:text=Some%20or%20all%20partners%20have,elements%20of%20partnerships%20and%20corporations)]{.underline}.

- **Limited Company (Ltd):** A private limited company is a separate
  legal entity owned by shareholders and run by directors. This
  structure provides **limited liability** -- owners (shareholders) are
  responsible for business debts only up to the amount they invested;
  their personal assets are protected in
  insolvency[[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/set-up-business#:~:text=Sole%20trader%20Limited%20company%20Legal,Directors%20need%20to%20follow)[companieshouse.blog.gov.uk](https://companieshouse.blog.gov.uk/2018/07/18/choosing-the-right-business-structure/#:~:text=Incorporating%20a%20limited%20liability%20company,Ltd)]{.underline}.
  To set up an Ltd, you must **incorporate** it with Companies House
  (register the company before starting
  business)[[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/set-up-business#:~:text=Registering%20You%20can%20start%20trading,for%20example)[companieshouse.blog.gov.uk](https://companieshouse.blog.gov.uk/2018/07/18/choosing-the-right-business-structure/#:~:text=Company%20directors%20run%20the%20company,You%20can%20be%20both)]{.underline},
  choosing a unique company name and filing constitutional documents. An
  Ltd has more ongoing duties: it must keep company accounts, file
  annual accounts and a confirmation statement (annual return) to
  Companies House, and follow corporate governance
  rules[[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/limited-company-formation#:~:text=Keeping%20records%20and%20accounts)[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/confirmation-statement-guidance#:~:text=Every%20company%2C%20including%20dormant%20and,company%20is%20up%20to%20date)]{.underline}.
  **Profits are subject to Corporation Tax** (paid by the company), and
  if you take money out as a salary or dividends, you may have
  additional personal tax to
  pay[[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/set-up-business#:~:text=needs%20to%20register%20for%20VAT,The%20company%20can%20have%20employees)[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/limited-company-formation#:~:text=Taxes)]{.underline}.
  Many small businesses choose the Ltd route for the protection of
  limited liability and a professional image, but note it involves more
  administration (annual filings and public disclosure of
  accounts)[[companieshouse.blog.gov.uk](https://companieshouse.blog.gov.uk/2018/07/18/choosing-the-right-business-structure/#:~:text=For%3A)[companieshouse.blog.gov.uk](https://companieshouse.blog.gov.uk/2018/07/18/choosing-the-right-business-structure/#:~:text=,limited%20liability%20protection)]{.underline}.
  *(**Note:** In UK law, there is no exact equivalent of a U.S. "LLC" --
  a **private limited company (Ltd)** is the closest entity, providing
  limited liability for owners in a similar way to an LLC.)*

- **Public Limited Company (PLC):** A PLC is a larger company structure
  that can offer shares to the public. It requires a minimum £50,000
  share capital and must have at least two directors and a qualified
  company secretary. PLCs are subject to stricter regulation and must
  file audited accounts. This format is generally used by large
  enterprises intending to trade shares publicly, so it's less common
  for small or starting businesses.

- **Other Forms (Social Enterprise, etc.):** If your venture has
  specific goals, other structures exist. For example, a **social
  enterprise** or **community interest company (CIC)** is a special type
  of limited company designed for businesses with social
  objectives[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/set-up-business#:~:text=,51).
  There are also **unincorporated associations** (often used for clubs
  or volunteer groups) and options for registering an **overseas
  company** branch in the
  UK[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/set-up-business#:~:text=,51).
  These have particular rules and may not be relevant for a typical
  commercial business, but it's good to be aware they exist.

**Taxation and Filing Obligations While Running a Business**

No matter the structure, you will need to **pay the correct taxes and
file required returns on time**. Below is an overview of key tax
responsibilities and dates for UK businesses:

- **Income Tax and Self Assessment (for Sole Traders & Partnerships):**
  If you operate as a sole trader or a partner, you are **taxed as an
  individual** on your business's profits. You must register for **Self
  Assessment** with HMRC and file an annual personal tax return
  declaring your business
  income[[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader#:~:text=You%20can%20start%20trading%20straight,can%20choose%20to%20register%20earlier)[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader#:~:text=When%20you%20submit%20your%20Self,36%C2%A0based%20on%20your%20profits)]{.underline}.
  The UK tax year runs from 6 April to 5 April, and the **online Self
  Assessment return is due by 31 January** following the end of the tax
  year (for example, for the tax year ending 5 April 2025, the deadline
  is 31 January
  2026)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/self-assessment-tax-returns/deadlines#:~:text=Submitting%20an%20online%20tax%20return).
  If you file a paper return, the deadline is earlier (31
  October)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/self-assessment-tax-returns/deadlines#:~:text=Sending%20a%20paper%20tax%20return).
  **Payment of Income Tax** owed for the year is also due by 31 January,
  along with any Class 4 National Insurance (see below). Many sole
  traders and partners making substantial income will also need to make
  **payments on account** -- advance payments of next year's tax -- due
  by 31 January and 31 July each
  year[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/self-assessment-tax-returns/deadlines#:~:text=Paying%20the%20tax%20you%20owe).
  When first becoming self-employed, you should inform HMRC by 5 October
  following the end of the tax year in which you started (to get set up
  for Self
  Assessment)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/self-assessment-tax-returns/deadlines#:~:text=Telling%20HMRC%20you%20need%20to,complete%20a%20return).
  Through the Self Assessment process, HMRC will calculate your Income
  Tax and National Insurance based on profits you
  report[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader#:~:text=When%20you%20submit%20your%20Self,36%C2%A0based%20on%20your%20profits).
  Keep in mind that sole traders and partners can deduct allowable
  business expenses and may qualify for certain **tax reliefs** to
  reduce the tax
  bill[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader#:~:text=There%20may%20be%20other%20taxes,you%20as%20a%20sole%20trader).

- **National Insurance Contributions (NICs):** In addition to income
  tax, self-employed people pay National Insurance to qualify for state
  benefits and pension. Sole traders and partners pay **Class 2 NIC** (a
  small weekly flat rate) if their profits exceed a small threshold, and
  **Class 4 NIC** (a percentage of annual profits over a
  threshold)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/set-up-business#:~:text=National%20Insurance%20You%20may%20need,Corporation%20Tax%20on%20any%20profits).
  (If your profits are below the Class 2 threshold, you can choose to
  pay Class 2 voluntarily to protect your State Pension
  record[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/set-up-business#:~:text=National%20Insurance%20You%20may%20need,Corporation%20Tax%20on%20any%20profits).)
  For example, Class 2 is a fixed weekly amount (HMRC notifies the exact
  rate each year, around £3-£4/week), and Class 4 is a percentage
  (around 9--10%) on profits above \~£12,000 (exact thresholds vary by
  year). These NICs are calculated and due as part of your Self
  Assessment
  bill[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader#:~:text=When%20you%20submit%20your%20Self,36%C2%A0based%20on%20your%20profits).
  If you run a **limited company**, the company's profits are not
  subject to NIC, but any wages you pay yourself or employees *are* --
  see "Employing People" below. Company directors who draw a salary are
  treated as employees for NIC purposes (usually paying Class 1 NIC
  through the PAYE
  system)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/set-up-business#:~:text=National%20Insurance%20You%20may%20need,Corporation%20Tax%20on%20any%20profits).

- **Corporation Tax and Company Accounts (for Limited Companies):** A
  limited company must pay **Corporation Tax** on its profits. The
  current Corporation Tax rate is applied to the company's taxable
  profits (rate as of 2025 depends on profit level, with a main rate of
  25%, but a small profits rate of 19% for smaller profits -- these
  rates can change with budgets). Key deadlines: after your company's
  financial year-end, you must **file a Company Tax Return (CT600)
  within 12 months** of the end of the accounting period, and **pay any
  Corporation Tax due within 9 months and 1 day** after the period
  ends[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/prepare-file-annual-accounts-for-limited-company#:~:text=File%20first%20accounts%20with%20Companies,period%20for%20Corporation%20Tax%20ends).
  For example, if your company's financial year ends 31 March 2025, the
  Corporation Tax payment is due by 1 January
  2026[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/prepare-file-annual-accounts-for-limited-company#:~:text=File%20first%20accounts%20with%20Companies,period%20for%20Corporation%20Tax%20ends).
  Company accounting periods are usually 12 months (they align with your
  financial
  year)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/prepare-file-annual-accounts-for-limited-company#:~:text=Your%20accounting%20period%20for%20Corporation,covered%20by%20your%20annual%20accounts).
  Separately, you must also **file annual accounts** with Companies
  House -- generally within **9 months after the company year-end** (or
  21 months from incorporation for the first
  accounts)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/prepare-file-annual-accounts-for-limited-company#:~:text=Action%20Deadline%20File%20first%20accounts,period%20for%20Corporation%20Tax%20ends).
  These accounts may need to be audited if the company is above certain
  size thresholds, but small companies can file abridged or unaudited
  accounts. Missing a filing deadline can result in penalties from both
  Companies House and
  HMRC[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/prepare-file-annual-accounts-for-limited-company#:~:text=,if%20you%20%2036).
  In addition to accounts, every company must file a **confirmation
  statement** at least once a year to confirm that Companies House has
  the correct information about the company (such as directors,
  registered office,
  shareholders)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/confirmation-statement-guidance#:~:text=Every%20company%2C%20including%20dormant%20and,company%20is%20up%20to%20date).
  The confirmation statement is due within 14 days of the end of each
  12-month review
  period[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/confirmation-statement-guidance#:~:text=Review%20period)
  -- in practice, usually once a year around the anniversary of
  incorporation. **Directors' tax:** If you are a director-shareholder,
  you might pay yourself a combination of salary (which is deductible
  for company tax and taxed via PAYE) and dividends (which are paid from
  post-tax profits and have their own dividend tax rates).
  Directors/shareholders need to report dividends on Self Assessment
  returns and may owe Income Tax on them depending on the amount. In
  summary, running an Ltd means handling two layers of tax filings --
  the company's Corporation Tax and your own personal tax if you draw
  income from the
  company[[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/limited-company-formation#:~:text=Taxes)[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/set-up-business#:~:text=Income%20Tax%20You%20may%20need,dividends%20or%20a%20directors%E2%80%99%20loan)]{.underline}.

- **Value Added Tax (VAT):** VAT is a tax on sales of most goods and
  services. **Any business, whatever the structure, must register for
  VAT if its annual taxable turnover exceeds the registration
  threshold**. As of April 2024, the VAT registration threshold is
  **£90,000** in a rolling 12-month period (previously
  £85,000)[[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/vat-increasing-the-registration-and-deregistration-thresholds/increasing-the-vat-registration-threshold#:~:text=2024)[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/register-for-vat#:~:text=You%20must%20register%20if%20either%3A)]{.underline}.
  You must monitor your sales and register within 30 days of the end of
  any month in which your past 12 months' turnover went over £90k, or if
  you expect to exceed £90k in the next 30 days
  alone[[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/register-for-vat#:~:text=You%20must%20register%20if%20either%3A)[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/register-for-vat#:~:text=If%20you%20exceeded%20the%20threshold,in%20the%20last%2012%20months)]{.underline}.
  Once registered, you need to charge VAT on your sales (output tax) and
  can reclaim VAT on your business purchases (input tax). VAT returns
  are usually filed quarterly (though some businesses file monthly or
  annually), and payment of any net VAT due is typically required one
  month and 7 days after each VAT quarter ends. Many small businesses
  choose to register for VAT voluntarily even below the threshold,
  especially if they sell mostly to VAT-registered customers (so they
  can reclaim VAT on
  costs)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader#:~:text=You%20need%20to%20register%20for,you%20want%20to%20%2038).
  If you *only* sell VAT-exempt goods/services (or qualify for specific
  exemptions), you may not need to
  register[[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/register-for-vat#:~:text=You%20must%20pay%20HM%20Revenue,the%20date%20they%20register%20you)[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/register-for-vat#:~:text=You%20do%20not%20have%20to,of%20scope%E2%80%99%20goods%20and%20services)]{.underline}.
  **VAT accounting** can be complex -- you must keep proper VAT records
  and submit VAT returns online (Making Tax Digital rules require
  digital record-keeping and submission). Failing to register or
  late-registering when required can result in backdated VAT and
  penalties[[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/register-for-vat#:~:text=You%20must%20register%20if%20your,12%20months%20goes%20over%20%C2%A390%2C000)[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/register-for-vat#:~:text=Late%20registration)]{.underline},
  so keep an eye on your turnover. Once registered, if your turnover
  later falls, you can deregister if your taxable turnover goes below
  the deregistration threshold (£88,000 from April
  2024)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/vat-increasing-the-registration-and-deregistration-thresholds/increasing-the-vat-registration-threshold#:~:text=2024).

- **Employing People: PAYE, NI, and Pensions:** If your business hires
  employees (even if you hire yourself as a director on payroll), you
  take on additional tax obligations. You must operate **PAYE (Pay As
  You Earn)** payroll to deduct Income Tax and National Insurance from
  employees' wages and remit them to HMRC. This involves registering as
  an employer with HMRC and reporting payroll information in real time
  (RTI) every payday. **Employer's National Insurance**: you as an
  employer must pay Class 1 secondary National Insurance contributions
  on your employees' earnings above the NI threshold (this is in
  addition to the NIC you deduct from their
  pay)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader#:~:text=,workplace%20pensions%20to%20eligible%20staff).
  Small employers can claim an **Employment Allowance** which reduces
  the employer NIC bill by up to £5,000 per
  year[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader#:~:text=,workplace%20pensions%20to%20eligible%20staff).
  Payroll taxes (Income Tax and NIC) are usually due monthly -- by the
  22nd of the month after payday if paying electronically. Moreover, by
  law you need to provide eligible workers with a **workplace pension**:
  **every employer in the UK must automatically enrol certain staff into
  a pension scheme and contribute to
  it**[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/workplace-pensions/joining-a-workplace-pension#:~:text=All%20employers%20must%20provide%20a,This%20is%20called%20%E2%80%98automatic%20enrolment%E2%80%99).
  This is called **Automatic Enrolment**, and it applies to all
  employees aged 22 to State Pension Age who earn above £10,000 per
  year[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/workplace-pensions/joining-a-workplace-pension#:~:text=contributions%20to%20your%20pension%20if,all%20of%20the%20following%20apply).
  If you have any staff meeting those criteria, you must enroll them and
  contribute at least the minimum required percentage (currently
  employers contribute a minimum of 3% and the total minimum
  contribution is 8% of qualifying earnings, with the employee making up
  the
  rest)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/workplace-pensions-and-automatic-enrolment-employers-perspectives-2022/workplace-pensions-and-automatic-enrolment-employers-perspectives-2022#:~:text=made%20by%20employees%20to%20their,Continue%20paying).
  You also have ongoing **pension duties**: monitoring ages and earnings
  of staff (if someone's pay rises above £10k or they turn 22, you must
  enroll them), handling opt-outs/opt-ins, keeping records, and
  re-enrolling any opted-out employees every 3
  years[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/workplace-pensions-and-automatic-enrolment-employers-perspectives-2022/workplace-pensions-and-automatic-enrolment-employers-perspectives-2022#:~:text=employer,the%20scheme%20and%20keep%20records).
  After hiring your first employee, The Pensions Regulator will require
  you to complete a declaration of compliance for auto-enrolment. In
  addition, when employing staff you must comply with various
  **employment laws** -- for example, paying at least the National
  Minimum Wage/National Living Wage, providing statutory minimum paid
  holidays, ensuring a safe workplace, and not discriminating. (The
  **National Minimum Wage** is the legal floor for hourly pay; it varies
  by age -- employers *must* pay workers at least the minimum wage for
  their age
  group[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/workplace-pensions/joining-a-workplace-pension#:~:text=,guidance%20if%20you%E2%80%99re%20not%20sure).)
  You are also **legally required to have Employers' Liability
  Insurance** (with minimum £5 million cover) in most cases when you
  have employees -- this insurance covers you if an employee is injured
  or falls ill due to their work. Overall, taking on employees brings
  significant responsibilities, so be prepared to run payroll and meet
  HR
  requirements[[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader#:~:text=There%20are%C2%A0things%20you%E2%80%99ll%20need%20to,You%E2%80%99ll%20have%20more%20responsibilities%2C%20including)[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/limited-company-formation#:~:text=There%20are%C2%A0things%20you%E2%80%99ll%20need%20to,You%E2%80%99ll%20have%20more%20responsibilities%2C%20including)]{.underline}.

- **Other Taxes:** Depending on your business activities, there may be
  additional taxes or duties. For example, if your business owns
  commercial property, you may be liable for **Business Rates** (a local
  property tax). Small businesses can often get rates relief -- many
  small premises qualify for 100% relief, meaning they pay no
  rates[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader#:~:text=,your%20business%20from).
  If you operate in specific sectors (like alcohol production, fuel
  sales, waste management, etc.), there could be excise duties or
  environmental taxes. If you import or export goods, be aware of
  customs duties and import VAT. While these specialized taxes won't
  apply to every business, it's important to **check industry-specific
  requirements** when you start (HMRC provides guidance by industry via
  its "Check what taxes you may need to register for"
  tool)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader#:~:text=business%20expenses%20).

**Keeping up with deadlines:** It's critical to mark your calendar with
all relevant tax dates to avoid penalties. To summarize the big ones:
**31 January** each year for personal Self Assessment returns and tax
payments[[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/self-assessment-tax-returns/deadlines#:~:text=Submitting%20an%20online%20tax%20return)[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/self-assessment-tax-returns/deadlines#:~:text=Paying%20the%20tax%20you%20owe)]{.underline};
for companies, usually **9 months after year-end** for accounts filing
and tax
payment[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/prepare-file-annual-accounts-for-limited-company#:~:text=File%20first%20accounts%20with%20Companies,period%20for%20Corporation%20Tax%20ends)
(exact date depends on your chosen year-end); VAT returns (if
applicable) typically due a month after each quarter; payroll reporting
due each pay date (with monthly remittances by 22nd); and pension
contributions must be paid to the scheme by the regulatory deadlines
(usually by 22nd of the following month). Always file on time -- HMRC
and Companies House impose fines for late filing of returns, accounts,
or confirmation
statements[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/prepare-file-annual-accounts-for-limited-company#:~:text=,if%20you%20%2036).
If circumstances make you miss a deadline, contact the authorities
proactively; for example, you can apply for an extension on filing
company accounts in some cases if
needed[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/prepare-file-annual-accounts-for-limited-company#:~:text=Filing%20your%20accounts%20and%20tax,return).

**Other Responsibilities When Running a Business**

Beyond taxes, running a business in the UK entails various **legal and
administrative obligations**. Key things to keep in mind include:

- **Registering and Record-Keeping:** You must ensure you're properly
  **registered**: sole traders and partners register with HMRC for Self
  Assessment, and limited companies/LLPs register with Companies House
  (and also with HMRC for Corporation Tax after incorporation). From day
  one of trading, keep **accurate financial records** (sales, expenses,
  invoices, receipts,
  etc.)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader#:~:text=Keeping%20records%20and%20accounts).
  Good record-keeping isn't just best practice -- it's a legal
  requirement for tax and, for companies, for company law. Records will
  support your tax returns and accounts, and HMRC can charge penalties
  if records are inadequate. Typically, business records must be
  retained for at least 5-6 years. If you're VAT-registered or an
  employer, there are specific records you must maintain (VAT accounts,
  payroll records,
  etc.)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/limited-company-formation#:~:text=Keeping%20records%20and%20accounts).

- **Business Name and Stationery:** Choose a compliant **business
  name**. Sole traders can trade under their own personal name or a
  chosen name, but must avoid certain sensitive words or implying a
  legal form they are not (e.g. a sole trader cannot use "Ltd" in their
  name)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/set-up-business#:~:text=yourself%20a%20salary%2C%20dividends%20or,You%20can%20choose%20to%20register).
  Limited companies must have a unique name ending in "Limited" or "Ltd"
  and follow naming rules (avoiding offensive terms or names too similar
  to existing
  companies)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/limited-company-formation#:~:text=Naming%20your%20business).
  If you use a trading name different from your official name, you must
  include your own name or company name on official paperwork. Companies
  and LLPs have to display their registered name and number on letters,
  order forms, websites, etc. (as required by the Companies Act).

- **Licenses and Permits:** Depending on your business activities, you
  may need specific **licenses or permits**. For example, selling
  alcohol requires a premises and personal licence, serving food may
  require a hygiene certificate, playing music in a venue needs a music
  license, street trading or outdoor signage might need local council
  permission, etc. Always **check the regulations for your industry** --
  operating without a required license can lead to fines or
  closure[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader#:~:text=You%20may%20have%20other%20responsibilities,on%20what%20your%20business%20does).
  Gov.uk's Business Licensing tool can help identify if your business
  needs any permits. Also, some professions/trades require memberships
  or certifications (e.g. solicitors, medical practitioners, private
  security, etc.). Ensure you comply with any such requirements before
  trading.

- **Insurance:** Some insurances are compulsory. If you hire staff,
  Employers' Liability Insurance is required by law (with few
  exceptions) as mentioned. If you use vehicles for business, you must
  have appropriate motor insurance for business use. Even when not
  compulsory, certain **business insurances** are highly recommended --
  for instance, Public Liability Insurance to cover claims from the
  public, Professional Indemnity Insurance if you provide advice or
  services, or Product Liability if you manufacture
  goods[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader#:~:text=Sole%20trader%20businesses%20have%20%E2%80%98unlimited,get%20more%20protection%20with%C2%A0business%20insurance).
  Insurance can protect you against unexpected events and is often a
  prudent part of risk management for a business (indeed, government
  guidance suggests insurance can offer sole traders more protection,
  since they don't have limited
  liability)[[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader#:~:text=Legal%20risks%20or%20%E2%80%98liability%E2%80%99)[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader#:~:text=Sole%20trader%20businesses%20have%20%E2%80%98unlimited,get%20more%20protection%20with%C2%A0business%20insurance)]{.underline}.

- **Premises and Zoning:** If you run your business from home, check if
  there are any restrictions (rental agreements, mortgage terms, or
  local council regulations might require permission for home
  businesses)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader#:~:text=Where%20you%20work).
  If you operate from commercial premises, you'll likely be subject to
  **business rates** (as noted earlier). Many small businesses can get
  **Small Business Rate Relief**, which can reduce or eliminate the
  rates bill for qualifying
  properties[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader#:~:text=,your%20business%20from).
  If you rent a workspace, factor in the rent, utility contracts, and
  any service charges, and ensure the usage class of the property is
  suitable for your business (planning permission may be needed to
  change how a premises is used). You also must comply with health and
  safety rules for your workplace -- the extent depends on your business
  type (e.g. fire safety checks, risk assessments, providing first aid
  kit, etc., especially if you have employees or customers on site).

- **Trading Standards and Consumer Law:** When dealing with customers,
  you must follow fair trading laws. The **Consumer Rights Act**
  requires that goods be as described and of satisfactory quality, and
  services be provided with reasonable care and skill. If you sell
  online or off-premises (e.g. door-to-door or at markets), consumers
  generally have cancellation rights (a 14-day "cooling-off" period for
  many online sales) -- you need to provide required information and
  honor refunds as mandated. If you **sell goods online**, there are
  specific regulations (such as displaying terms and conditions, privacy
  policy, compliance with distance selling
  rules)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader#:~:text=There%20are%20also%20rules%20you,must%20follow%20if%20you).
  Also, **data protection** laws (UK GDPR and Data Protection Act) apply
  if you collect or store personal information about customers or
  employees. You may need to register with the Information
  Commissioner's Office (ICO) and put proper data-handling measures in
  place[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader#:~:text=,45).

- **Intellectual Property (IP):** Ensure you respect IP rights and
  protect your own. Choosing a unique name or logo? Check trademarks to
  avoid infringing someone else's
  brand[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/set-up-business#:~:text=yourself%20a%20salary%2C%20dividends%20or,You%20can%20choose%20to%20register).
  You can register your own trademark for your brand name or logo if
  needed. Likewise, consider if you need to patent any inventions or
  protect designs. Gov.uk has an **Intellectual Property Office**
  guidance on safeguarding your
  IP[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/set-up-business#:~:text=,Intellectual%20property%20and%20your%20work).
  While IP may not be an immediate concern for all new businesses, it's
  important if you have a novel product, creative content, or branding
  you want to secure.

- **Continuing Compliance:** Running a business is an ongoing process of
  compliance. For companies, this means holding on to important records
  (like minutes of meetings, records of shareholders and directors,
  etc.), **updating Companies House** whenever certain changes occur
  (such as change of directors or registered office) in addition to the
  annual confirmation
  statement[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/limited-company-formation#:~:text=Keeping%20records%20and%20accounts).
  For all businesses, it means **responding to correspondence** from
  HMRC or other regulators in a timely manner. For example, HMRC might
  open an inquiry into a tax return; Companies House will send reminders
  for accounts and confirmation statements. Staying organized and
  seeking professional advice (from accountants, solicitors, etc.) when
  needed can help you meet all these obligations. The government
  provides help for small businesses -- you can sign up for HMRC's
  business email updates or webinars for education on tax
  responsibilities[[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/become-sole-trader#:~:text=Step%207%3A%20Get%20help%20and,support)[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/limited-company-formation#:~:text=Help%20and%20support)]{.underline}.

**Selling or Exiting Your Business**

If you decide to **sell your business** (or close it down), there are
important steps and obligations to address so you finalize everything
legally and tax-efficiently. These differ slightly based on your
business structure:

- **Sole Trader -- Selling the Business:** When a sole trader sells
  their business (e.g. selling the business assets, goodwill, client
  list, etc. to a new owner), you'll need to **settle your final tax
  matters and inform HMRC**. There is an online form you can use to
  notify HMRC that you have ceased trading (this covers stopping Self
  Assessment and Class 2
  NIC)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/selling-your-business-your-responsibilities#:~:text=Telling%20HMRC).
  You must file a final Self Assessment tax return for the tax year in
  which you ceased trading, **entering the date you stopped trading on
  the
  return**[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/selling-your-business-your-responsibilities#:~:text=Tax%20returns).
  This return (due by the usual 31 Jan deadline) will include your final
  business profits up to the cessation date. If you were registered for
  VAT, you can request to **transfer your VAT registration number** to
  the new owner as part of the business sale, which can simplify matters
  for
  them[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/selling-your-business-your-responsibilities#:~:text=VAT%20registration).
  It's crucial to also notify any employees in advance -- if you had
  staff, you must tell them *when and why* you're selling and discuss
  any redundancy or transfer arrangements (so you don't breach their
  rights)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/selling-your-business-your-responsibilities#:~:text=Staff).
  Under UK law, when a business changes ownership, employees may be
  protected by TUPE regulations (which ensure that employees' terms and
  conditions transfer to the buyer, if it's a sale of an ongoing
  business). Additionally, selling your business might trigger a
  **Capital Gains Tax (CGT)** event. As a sole trader, if you sell
  business assets or goodwill, the money you receive (minus any
  allowable costs) could be a capital
  gain[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/selling-your-business-your-responsibilities#:~:text=You%20may%20have%20made%20a,the%20business%20that%20you%20keep).
  You should calculate any gain and report it on your tax return. The
  good news is you may be eligible for **Entrepreneurs' Relief**
  (formally known as **Business Asset Disposal Relief**) if you meet the
  conditions, which **reduces the CGT rate to 10%** on qualifying
  business
  disposals[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/selling-your-business-your-responsibilities#:~:text=You%20may%20have%20made%20a,the%20business%20that%20you%20keep).
  For example, if you've owned the business for at least 2 years and are
  selling it off completely, this relief can apply (with a lifetime
  limit of £1 million in gains per individual under current rules).
  There are other CGT reliefs that might reduce the tax due, so consider
  consulting a tax adviser or accountant when planning a
  sale[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/selling-your-business-your-responsibilities#:~:text=You%20may%20have%20made%20a,the%20business%20that%20you%20keep).
  In summary, as a sole trader you must: inform HMRC of cessation,
  complete final tax returns, pay any outstanding taxes (including CGT
  on gains from the sale), cancel your Class 2 NIC (HMRC's National
  Insurance helpline can assist with stopping NIC
  bills)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/selling-your-business-your-responsibilities#:~:text=Telling%20HMRC),
  and handle any employee issues. Once that's done, you can enjoy the
  proceeds of the sale knowing you've met your obligations.

- **Partnership -- Selling the Business or Your Stake:** In a
  partnership, you might be selling **your share of the partnership** or
  the partnership as a whole may be sold/dissolved. First, check the
  **partnership agreement** for any clauses about selling -- it may
  require certain approvals or have buy-out provisions for
  partners[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/selling-your-business-your-responsibilities/business-partnership#:~:text=Your%20responsibilities%20when%20selling%20a,depend%20on%20whether%20you%E2%80%99re%20selling).
  If *only one partner (you)* is selling their stake (and the
  partnership will continue with remaining/new partners), you will
  essentially **retire from the partnership**. You'd inform HMRC that
  you stopped being self-employed (cancelling Class 2 NIC if
  appropriate)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/selling-your-business-your-responsibilities/business-partnership#:~:text=If%20you%E2%80%99re%20stopping%20self),
  and you must still file your personal Self Assessment for the final
  period of trading up to the date you left the
  partnership[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/selling-your-business-your-responsibilities/business-partnership#:~:text=Tax%20returns).
  The partnership itself continues if at least two partners remain, so
  the nominated partner would continue to file the partnership tax
  return as normal (reflecting your final profit share up to your leave
  date). If instead **the entire partnership business is sold off or
  wound up**, then similar steps to a sole trader apply but for each
  partner. The **nominated partner must submit a final Partnership Tax
  Return by the usual deadline** (if the business ceased in 2024/25, the
  return for that year is due 31 Jan 2026 online, for
  instance)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/selling-your-business-your-responsibilities/business-partnership#:~:text=You%20must%3A),
  and each partner files their final personal tax return including the
  final share of
  profits[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/selling-your-business-your-responsibilities/business-partnership#:~:text=You%20must%3A).
  As with sole traders, if the partnership had employees, you must
  inform them of the business sale and handle any redundancies or
  transfers
  properly[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/selling-your-business-your-responsibilities/business-partnership#:~:text=Staff).
  Each partner should consider **Capital Gains Tax** on their portion of
  the sale proceeds -- for example, if the partnership's assets or
  goodwill are sold, each partner is treated as disposing of their
  fractional share. Entrepreneurs' Relief (Business Asset Disposal
  Relief) can also apply to partners who sell or dissolve their
  business, potentially qualifying for the 10% CGT rate on their
  gains[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/selling-your-business-your-responsibilities/business-partnership#:~:text=Capital%20Gains%20Tax)
  (conditions include that the partner was in the business for at least
  2 years, etc.). **Important:** If the partnership is simply changing
  members (one partner leaves or a new one joins) but the business
  continues, that isn't a full "disposal" of the whole business -- but
  the departing partner might still have a CGT event if they receive
  payment for their share. It's advisable to get professional advice in
  a partnership sale to allocate the sale price between assets
  (goodwill, etc.) and to handle things like treatment of any
  partnership debts. In any case, HMRC needs to be informed and final
  taxes paid. Also note, if you were paying Class 2 NIC as a
  self-employed partner, call HMRC to cancel it when you cease
  self-employment[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/selling-your-business-your-responsibilities/business-partnership#:~:text=If%20you%E2%80%99re%20stopping%20self).

- **Limited Company -- Selling the Company or Its Business:** With a
  limited company, there are two ways a business can be sold: **selling
  the company's shares** (you sell your ownership of the company to a
  buyer who takes over), or the company itself sells its assets/business
  (the company gets the proceeds, perhaps to distribute to
  shareholders). Each has different implications:

  - *Selling the entire company (share sale):* Here, you as the
    shareholder sell your shares to the buyer. Legally, the company
    entity remains the same -- only ownership changes. In this scenario,
    ensure a smooth handover of company management. If you are a
    director, you'll likely **resign as director** once the new owners
    take over, but **appoint any new directors before you resign** so
    the company is never without an official
    director[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/selling-your-business-your-responsibilities/limited-company#:~:text=Appoint%20new%20directors%20or%20company,secretaries).
    You must notify Companies House of the changes in directors (and any
    company secretaries) and update the register of **People with
    Significant Control** to reflect the new
    owner[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/selling-your-business-your-responsibilities/limited-company#:~:text=Appoint%20new%20directors%20or%20company,secretaries).
    After the sale, *you personally* may have a Capital Gains Tax
    liability on the sale of your shares (the difference between what
    you paid for the shares and what you sold them
    for)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/selling-your-business-your-responsibilities/limited-company#:~:text=Capital%20Gains%20Tax).
    Entrepreneurs' Relief (BADR) may reduce the CGT on qualifying share
    sales, too (commonly available if it was your personal company and
    you were an officer/employee and held the shares for 2+
    years)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/selling-your-business-your-responsibilities/limited-company#:~:text=Capital%20Gains%20Tax).
    You would claim that via your Self Assessment. Also, if you had
    previously given any **personal guarantees or secured personal
    assets for company debts**, those need attention. For instance, if
    you secured a company loan with a mortgage on your house or other
    personal collateral, you must inform the lender within **21 days of
    the sale** that you have sold the
    company[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/selling-your-business-your-responsibilities/limited-company#:~:text=Charges%20against%20your%20company).
    The lender may want the loan repaid or renegotiated with the new
    owners since your security was tied to your ownership. In terms of
    HMRC, selling shares does not directly require notifying HMRC in the
    same way as ceasing self-employment (since the company continues to
    exist), but you should ensure a clear break in terms of resigning as
    director and handing over accounting records, etc. The company will
    continue to have to file accounts and tax returns, now under the new
    ownership. If the company was VAT-registered, you might choose to
    **transfer the VAT registration** to the new owner's entity if they
    bought the business as a going
    concern[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/selling-your-business-your-responsibilities/limited-company#:~:text=VAT%20registration)
    (often in a share sale, the VAT registration just stays with the
    company since the company hasn't changed). One administrative task
    is to cancel your **PAYE scheme** as an employer if the company will
    no longer pay any staff under your tenure (though if the business
    continues, the PAYE scheme likely continues too). It's also
    courteous (and often contractually required) to inform major
    suppliers, clients, etc., of the change of ownership after the deal.

  - *Company selling its assets/business:* In this case, the company
    (under your ownership) sells some or all of its business assets to a
    buyer (which could be another company). If the **entire business
    operation is sold** -- often called a business transfer -- employees
    who are part of that business will typically be protected by TUPE
    law, meaning they *transfer* to the buyer on their existing terms.
    You as the seller must **inform and consult employees** about the
    transfer, including when and why it's happening and any effects on
    them (like relocation or redundancy if
    applicable)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/selling-your-business-your-responsibilities/limited-company#:~:text=If%20any%20employees%20are%20affected,them%20about%20the%20changes%2C%20including).
    Failing to inform employees properly can breach their rights, so
    this is vital. After an asset sale, your company will receive the
    sale proceeds. The company might then distribute the money to you
    (for example, via dividends or eventually liquidating the company)
    -- each route has its own tax implications. The company may owe
    **Corporation Tax on any gains** from selling assets (for instance,
    selling a piece of equipment or a property for more than its tax
    value creates a taxable gain for the company). If the company is
    going to continue in some form, it just pays any taxes due and
    carries on (maybe with a new line of business). If you intend to
    **close the company** after selling its business, you might go
    through a members' voluntary liquidation to distribute the remaining
    funds; in that case, **Business Asset Disposal Relief/Entrepreneurs'
    Relief** might be available on the final distribution to
    shareholders (treating it as capital gain on shares). Selling assets
    can be complex, so professional tax advice is recommended to
    structure the deal efficiently. Remember to also address things like
    cancelling any licenses/permits you no longer need and terminating
    contracts or leases if the buyer isn't taking them on. If the
    company will become essentially inactive (dormant) after selling its
    business, you must still continue to file annual
    accounts/confirmation statements until it's formally dissolved or
    transferred. If you do decide to **dissolve the company** post-sale,
    you must settle all HMRC liabilities first and then apply to strike
    off the company (or liquidate if it has significant assets left).

In all cases of selling a business, a few general points:

- Make sure employees are treated according to the law (redundancy pay
  if you're not transferring them, or proper notice of transfer if you
  are). Government guidance on **TUPE (Transfer of Undertakings)**
  explains how employees' jobs, terms, and continuity of employment are
  protected when a business changes
  hands[[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/selling-your-business-your-responsibilities#:~:text=If%20you%20have%20anyone%20working,you%2C%20you%20must%20tell%20them)[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/selling-your-business-your-responsibilities/limited-company#:~:text=If%20any%20employees%20are%20affected,them%20about%20the%20changes%2C%20including)]{.underline}.

- Finalize your **tax accounts** up to the point of sale. This often
  means doing a bit of extra work in your bookkeeping to clearly cut off
  trading at the handover date. Submit any final VAT return if you
  deregister, final payroll submissions if you stop being an employer,
  etc.

- If you're a sole trader or partner and you **keep any business
  assets** personally (instead of selling them), note that HMRC might
  treat it as if you sold those to yourself at market value for tax
  purposes. That could also trigger a capital gain or a balancing charge
  (for example, on a vehicle you claimed capital allowances on).

- **Notify all stakeholders**: besides HMRC and staff, inform your bank
  (you may need to close a business bank account), insurers (cancel or
  transfer policies), suppliers and customers (so they know who will be
  responsible moving forward or to settle final bills), and any
  professional bodies your business is part of.

Finally, consider that selling a business can be a complex project. HMRC
provides **help and support for businesses** and there are tax reliefs
to take advantage of, but it often helps to involve accountants or
solicitors to ensure everything is done correctly. Once everything is
wrapped up -- taxes paid, forms submitted, employees managed -- you can
step back knowing you have met all your obligations in running and
exiting your UK business. **Running a business** involves many moving
parts, but the UK government (HMRC, Companies House, etc.) provides
clear rules and guidance to follow. By staying informed of your **legal
structure's requirements, meeting tax deadlines, keeping proper records,
and fulfilling duties to any staff and customers**, you will keep your
business compliant and primed for success (and a smooth sale when the
time comes).
