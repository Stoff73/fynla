**Personal Investment Products in the UK -- Tax Treatment Overview**

Below is an overview of major personal investment products in the UK --
both current and legacy -- and their tax treatments. Each section
outlines how income (interest and dividends), capital gains, and
death/inheritance are treated, as well as any tax-deferral or relief
features and relevant allowances.

**Individual Savings Accounts (ISAs) *(incl. Cash ISA, Stocks & Shares
ISA, Innovative Finance ISA, Lifetime ISA, Junior ISA)***

- **Tax-Free Income:** All income earned within an ISA -- whether
  interest (e.g. from cash or bonds) or dividends (e.g. from stocks) --
  is completely free from Income
  Tax[[moneyhelper.org.uk](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/savings/investing/stocks-and-shares-isas#:~:text=Investments%20that%20pay%20interest%20,if%20held%20within%20an%20ISA)[moneyhelper.org.uk](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/savings/investing/stocks-and-shares-isas#:~:text=Everyone%20gets%20a%20%C2%A3500%20tax,tax%20year%20before%20paying%20tax)]{.underline}.
  Dividends received in an ISA do not count toward your dividend
  allowance[[moneyhelper.org.uk]{.underline}](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/savings/investing/stocks-and-shares-isas#:~:text=Everyone%20gets%20a%20%C2%A3500%20tax,tax%20year%20before%20paying%20tax),
  and interest in an ISA does not count toward your Personal Savings
  Allowance, since none of it is taxable.

- **Capital Gains Tax:** Investments held in an ISA are exempt from
  Capital Gains Tax (CGT). Any profit made on selling ISA investments is
  tax-free (and conversely, losses in an ISA cannot be used to offset
  gains outside the
  ISA)[[moneyhelper.org.uk]{.underline}](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/savings/investing/stocks-and-shares-isas#:~:text=free%20and%20won%E2%80%99t%20impact%20your,dividend%20allowance).
  ISAs act as a tax "wrapper" sheltering investments from both Income
  Tax and
  CGT[[moneyhelper.org.uk]{.underline}](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/savings/investing/stocks-and-shares-isas#:~:text=Stocks%20and%20shares%20ISAs).

- **Annual ISA Allowance:** Contributions to ISAs are made from post-tax
  income (no upfront tax relief), but growth and withdrawals are
  tax-free. The annual ISA subscription limit is **£20,000** (for the
  2025/26 tax year), which can be split among different ISA types (Cash,
  Stocks & Shares, Innovative Finance, and Lifetime
  ISA)[[moneyhelper.org.uk](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/savings/investing/stocks-and-shares-isas#:~:text=Stocks%20and%20shares%20ISAs)[moneyhelper.org.uk](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/savings/investing/stocks-and-shares-isas#:~:text=,investments%20you%20can%20put%20inside)]{.underline}.
  Unused ISA allowance cannot be carried over to future years.

- **Interest & Dividend Detail:** In a **Cash ISA**, interest accrues
  tax-free. In a **Stocks & Shares ISA**, most forms of income (bond
  interest, rental income from funds, etc.) are
  tax-free[[moneyhelper.org.uk]{.underline}](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/savings/investing/stocks-and-shares-isas#:~:text=Investments%20that%20pay%20interest%20,if%20held%20within%20an%20ISA),
  and dividends from shares are tax-free (with no further tax or
  dividend
  withholding)[[moneyhelper.org.uk]{.underline}](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/savings/investing/stocks-and-shares-isas#:~:text=Everyone%20gets%20a%20%C2%A3500%20tax,tax%20year%20before%20paying%20tax).
  This means that holding interest-bearing investments or
  dividend-paying equities inside an ISA provides 100% tax-free
  income[[moneyhelper.org.uk]{.underline}](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/savings/investing/stocks-and-shares-isas#:~:text=Investments%20that%20pay%20interest%20,if%20held%20within%20an%20ISA).

- **On Death -- Inheritance Tax:** Funds in an ISA remain **income and
  CGT free up until the date of closure after death**, but they **do
  form part of the estate for Inheritance Tax (IHT)**
  purposes[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/individual-savings-accounts/if-you-die#:~:text=There%20will%20be%20no%20Income,estate%20for%20Inheritance%20Tax%20purposes).
  When an ISA holder dies, the ISA's tax-free status can continue for up
  to 3 years (or until the estate is settled, if sooner) -- during this
  period no Income Tax or CGT applies to growth in the
  account[[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/individual-savings-accounts/if-you-die#:~:text=Otherwise%2C%20your%20ISA%20provider%20will,1%20day%20after%20you%20die)[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/individual-savings-accounts/if-you-die#:~:text=There%20will%20be%20no%20Income,estate%20for%20Inheritance%20Tax%20purposes)]{.underline}.
  However, the ISA value is counted in the estate for IHT. **Spousal
  Transfer:** A surviving spouse/civil partner can inherit an Additional
  Permitted Subscription (APS) -- essentially an extra ISA allowance
  equal to the deceased's ISA value -- allowing them to re-shelter those
  assets in their own
  ISA[[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/individual-savings-accounts/inheriting-an-isa-from-your-spouse-civil-partner#:~:text=If%20your%20spouse%20or%20civil,can%20inherit%20their%20ISA%20allowance)[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/individual-savings-accounts/inheriting-an-isa-from-your-spouse-civil-partner#:~:text=As%20well%20as%20your%20normal,free%20amount%20up%20to%20either)]{.underline}.
  This prevents loss of the ISA tax benefits for the surviving spouse.
  *(Note: If the ISA holds certain AIM stocks that qualify for Business
  Property Relief, those shares could be IHT-free after 2 years, but
  this is due to the nature of the investment, not the ISA wrapper
  itself.)* Importantly, death itself does **not** trigger any Income
  Tax or CGT on ISA
  holdings[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/individual-savings-accounts/if-you-die#:~:text=There%20will%20be%20no%20Income,estate%20for%20Inheritance%20Tax%20purposes),
  and the ISA's tax shelter ends only when the account is closed or
  after 3 years.

- **Legacy Accounts (PEPs, TESSAs, etc.):** ISAs were introduced in
  1999, replacing older tax-free accounts: **Personal Equity Plans
  (PEPs)** and **Tax-Exempt Special Savings Accounts (TESSAs)**.
  **PEPs** (available 1987--1999) allowed investments in stocks/funds
  with tax-free growth and income -- they were effectively the precursor
  to Stocks & Shares
  ISAs[[en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_Equity_Plan#:~:text=Growth%20in%20a%20PEP%20was,an%20annual%20allowance%20of%20%C2%A33%2C000)[en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_Equity_Plan#:~:text=)]{.underline}.
  PEPs became Stocks & Shares ISAs in 2008, and their tax advantages (no
  Income Tax on dividends, no CGT on gains) were grandfathered
  in[[en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_Equity_Plan#:~:text=Growth%20in%20a%20PEP%20was,an%20annual%20allowance%20of%20%C2%A33%2C000)[en.wikipedia.org](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_Equity_Plan#:~:text=Following%20the%20introduction%20of%20Individual,1)]{.underline}.
  **TESSAs** (1991--1999) were 5-year bank/building society accounts for
  cash savings; interest was tax-free if the account was held to
  maturity[[en.wikipedia.org]{.underline}](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax-exempt_special_savings_account#:~:text=National%20Insurance%20number%20%2C%20to,a%20clawback%20of%20the%20tax).
  The maximum total TESSA contribution was £9,000 over five
  years[[en.wikipedia.org]{.underline}](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax-exempt_special_savings_account#:~:text=investment%20could%20be%20operated%20by,58%20of%20the%20tax%20relief).
  Withdrawals of capital from a TESSA within the 5-year term would claw
  back the tax exemption on interest (though withdrawing the interest
  itself was allowed without losing
  relief)[[en.wikipedia.org]{.underline}](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax-exempt_special_savings_account#:~:text=National%20Insurance%20number%20%2C%20to,a%20clawback%20of%20the%20tax).
  After ISAs launched, maturing TESSA capital could be rolled into a
  special "TOISA" (TESSA-only ISA) to continue the tax-free
  status[[en.wikipedia.org]{.underline}](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax-exempt_special_savings_account#:~:text=TESSAs%20were%20replaced%20from%201999,funds%20transferred%20from%20another%20TOISA).
  **Help to Buy ISAs** (now closed to new savers) and **Lifetime ISAs
  (LISAs)** are variants of ISAs with a government bonus for first-home
  purchase or retirement. These, like all ISAs, offer tax-free
  interest/gains. **Junior ISAs (JISAs)** replaced Child Trust Funds
  (see below) and allow up to £9,000 a year to be saved for a child with
  tax-free investment
  growth[[nsandi.com](https://www.nsandi.com/tax-free-saving#:~:text=Image%3A%20Junior%20ISA)[nsandi.com](https://www.nsandi.com/tax-free-saving#:~:text=A%20tax%20free%20way%20to,to%20the%20age%20of%2018)]{.underline}.
  All these accounts -- past and present -- share the fundamental tax
  treatment of ISAs: no Income Tax or CGT on returns.

**Onshore Investment Bonds (UK Life Assurance Bonds)**

- **Description:** Onshore investment bonds are single-premium life
  insurance policies that serve as investment wrappers. They provide
  **tax-deferred growth**: the investments inside the bond can generate
  income and gains which are not taxed directly on the policyholder year
  by
  year[[taxscape.deloitte.com]{.underline}](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/insurance-bonds.aspx#:~:text=Ongoing%20taxation%20of%20the%20policyholder).
  Instead, taxation is deferred until a **chargeable event** occurs
  (such as full surrender, maturity, death of the life assured, or
  certain large
  withdrawals)[[taxscape.deloitte.com](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/insurance-bonds.aspx#:~:text=bond%20may%20be%20minimal%2C%20but,be%20subject%20to%20income%20tax)[taxscape.deloitte.com](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/insurance-bonds.aspx#:~:text=Chargeable%20event%20gains%20are%20calculated,gains%20which%20have%20been%20taxed)]{.underline}.

- **Income Tax Treatment:** When a chargeable event occurs, any **gain**
  on the bond is taxed as **income** (specifically as savings income) in
  that tax
  year[[taxscape.deloitte.com](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/insurance-bonds.aspx#:~:text=bond%20may%20be%20minimal%2C%20but,be%20subject%20to%20income%20tax)[taxscape.deloitte.com](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/insurance-bonds.aspx#:~:text=Taxation%20of%20chargeable%20event%20gains)]{.underline}.
  Notably, gains from life insurance bonds are not subject to CGT --
  they fall under income tax rules. The gain is added to the
  policyholder's income and taxed at their marginal Income Tax rate.
  However, UK (onshore) bonds come with a **tax credit at basic rate**:
  it is deemed that 20% tax has already been paid on the gain by the
  life fund (because the insurer pays corporation tax on investment
  returns inside the bond). This means **basic rate taxpayers have no
  further tax to pay on the bond gain**, and higher-rate or
  additional-rate taxpayers pay only the difference between their higher
  rate and basic
  rate[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/gains-on-foreign-life-insurance-policies-hs321-self-assessment-helpsheet/hs321-gains-on-foreign-life-insurance-policies-2025#:~:text=In%20these%20notes%20%E2%80%98gains%E2%80%99%20are,repayable%20basic%20rate%20tax%20credit).
  For example, a 40% taxpayer would pay an additional 20% on the gain.
  This **non-reclaimable 20% credit** applies only to UK onshore bonds
  (offshore bonds have no such credit -- see next
  section)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/gains-on-foreign-life-insurance-policies-hs321-self-assessment-helpsheet/hs321-gains-on-foreign-life-insurance-policies-2025#:~:text=In%20these%20notes%20%E2%80%98gains%E2%80%99%20are,repayable%20basic%20rate%20tax%20credit).

- **5% Tax-Deferred Withdrawals:** Onshore bonds allow policyholders to
  withdraw up to **5% of the original investment amount each policy
  year** without immediate tax
  liability[[taxscape.deloitte.com]{.underline}](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/insurance-bonds.aspx#:~:text=Withdrawals).
  These 5% withdrawals are treated as a return of capital (tax deferred)
  and can be taken for 20 years (5% × 20 = 100% of the principal).
  Unused 5% allowances roll over to future years (e.g. if you take no
  withdrawal in year 1, you could take 10% in year 2 with no immediate
  charge)[[taxscape.deloitte.com]{.underline}](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/insurance-bonds.aspx#:~:text=The%20policyholder%20can%20withdraw%205,2%20x%205).
  Importantly, these withdrawals are not tax-free but tax-deferred --
  they will reduce the cost basis of the bond, and any amounts in excess
  of the cumulative 5% allowances, or any gain remaining when the bond
  is fully surrendered, will be a taxable gain at that
  point[[taxscape.deloitte.com](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/insurance-bonds.aspx#:~:text=If%20withdrawals%20are%20made%20in,can%20be%20made%20more%20flexibly)[taxscape.deloitte.com](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/insurance-bonds.aspx#:~:text=Chargeable%20event%20gains%20are%20calculated,gains%20which%20have%20been%20taxed)]{.underline}.

- **No Personal Income During Accumulation:** There is no annual tax on
  interest, dividends or capital growth generated within the bond **to
  the policyholder** -- the bond's internal growth is taxed within the
  life fund at rates roughly equivalent to basic rate tax for UK bonds,
  but the investor doesn't declare anything on their personal tax return
  unless a chargeable event is
  triggered[[taxscape.deloitte.com]{.underline}](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/insurance-bonds.aspx#:~:text=Ongoing%20taxation%20of%20the%20policyholder).
  Thus, the bond provides tax deferral and can be advantageous for
  investors who plan to withdraw when they are in a lower tax bracket or
  assign segments to someone else (assignments as a gift do not trigger
  a tax charge).

- **Top-Slicing Relief:** If a bond is held for many years and then
  encashed, the gain realized in that final year can be large and
  potentially push the investor into a higher tax bracket. **Top-slicing
  relief** is a mechanism that HMRC provides to mitigate this "bunching"
  of
  income[[taxscape.deloitte.com](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/insurance-bonds.aspx#:~:text=Taxation%20of%20chargeable%20event%20gains)[taxscape.deloitte.com](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/insurance-bonds.aspx#:~:text=year%20before%20the%20chargeable%20event%2C,subject%20to%20higher%20rate%20tax)]{.underline}.
  It works by averaging the gain over the number of complete years the
  bond was held to determine an effective rate of tax, then applying
  that to the actual gain to calculate any excess tax due. In practice,
  top-slicing relief can reduce the rate of tax charged on the gain for
  long-held bonds, preventing an anomalously large one-off gain from
  unduly pushing the taxpayer into a higher rate
  band[[taxscape.deloitte.com](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/insurance-bonds.aspx#:~:text=Taxation%20of%20chargeable%20event%20gains)[taxscape.deloitte.com](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/insurance-bonds.aspx#:~:text=year%20before%20the%20chargeable%20event%2C,subject%20to%20higher%20rate%20tax)]{.underline}.
  The relief ensures fairness but does not reduce the gain itself -- it
  only affects the rate at which it is taxed.

- **Capital Gains:** Gains on life bonds are **not subject to Capital
  Gains Tax**. As noted, any profit is instead treated as income. This
  means the CGT annual exempt amount is not usable against bond gains,
  but also that bond gains won't use up your CGT allowance. There is a
  potential benefit in that bond gains do not count toward the
  investor's income for determining the Personal Savings Allowance or
  dividend allowance, but they do count for determining marginal Income
  Tax rates.

- **Dividend and Interest Income Inside the Bond:** The bond may invest
  in funds that produce dividends or interest, but the investor doesn't
  directly receive these. Inside an onshore bond, UK dividends come with
  a tax credit and interest is subject to the insurer's tax; effectively
  the life fund pays tax on income and gains at basic rate. The investor
  is only taxed on withdrawals/encashment, as described above. Thus,
  from the investor's perspective, there is no reportable dividend or
  interest income during the bond's life -- only the eventual gain.

- **Tax on Death:** If the bond policyholder (or the last life assured
  under the policy) dies, that is a **chargeable event**. The bond ends
  and a gain may be calculated as of the date of death (essentially the
  difference between the bond's value plus any withdrawals taken and the
  amount invested, similar to
  surrender)[[taxscape.deloitte.com]{.underline}](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/insurance-bonds.aspx#:~:text=Chargeable%20event%20gains%20are%20calculated,gains%20which%20have%20been%20taxed).
  Any such gain is **income-taxable** on the deceased's final tax return
  (or the beneficiary, depending on policy ownership). The insurer will
  issue a chargeable event certificate showing the gain. The timing rule
  is that the gain is treated as arising in the tax year of
  death[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/gains-on-uk-life-insurance-policies-hs320-self-assessment-helpsheet/hs320-gains-on-uk-life-insurance-policies-2025#:~:text=8).
  Importantly, death does **not** wipe out the income tax on the bond's
  gain (unlike some other investments where death resets cost basis for
  CGT -- here CGT isn't in point and income tax on the accrued gain is
  due). However, if the bond is written under trust, the gain would be
  assessed on the trustees or beneficiaries per specific rules. For
  Inheritance Tax, the **value of the bond** (the policy's surrender
  value at death) is included in the deceased's estate value for IHT
  purposes (there is no *inherent* IHT relief for holding a life bond).
  Many investors mitigate this by writing the bond into a trust from the
  outset, so that on death the bond's value is outside their estate for
  IHT -- but absent such planning, the bond's value is IHT-chargeable
  like any other investment asset. There is **no special IHT exemption**
  for investment bonds themselves.

- **Relevant Allowances/Reliefs:** Aside from the 5% deferred withdrawal
  allowance and top-slicing relief, there are no personal annual
  allowances specific to bond gains (since they aren't dividends or
  standard interest). However, bond gains do fall under the category of
  *savings income* for Income Tax, meaning that in some cases a portion
  of a bond gain could be covered by the savings nil-rate bands (the
  **£5,000 starting rate for savings** if one has low other income, and
  the **Personal Savings Allowance** of £1,000 for basic rate or £500
  for higher rate taxpayers). In practice, for onshore bonds the
  notional 20% tax credit complicates this slightly: a basic-rate
  taxpayer's entire gain is covered by the
  credit[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/gains-on-foreign-life-insurance-policies-hs321-self-assessment-helpsheet/hs321-gains-on-foreign-life-insurance-policies-2025#:~:text=In%20these%20notes%20%E2%80%98gains%E2%80%99%20are,repayable%20basic%20rate%20tax%20credit),
  and higher/additional rate taxpayers would report the gain (net of the
  basic rate credit) which could then potentially be offset by any
  available savings allowance. This is a complex area, but broadly,
  small bond gains might not incur further tax if within those savings
  thresholds.

- **Legacy note:** Onshore bonds have been a staple tax-planning product
  for many years. The tax regime described (5% rule, top slicing, etc.)
  has remained relatively constant. Do note that these are
  **non-qualifying life policies**, so they don't benefit from the older
  "qualifying policy" exemption (which applies to certain endowments
  with 10+ year premiums). Thus, any gain on an investment bond is
  chargeable in the manner described, rather than being tax-free.

**Offshore Investment Bonds (International Bonds)**

- **Description:** Offshore bonds are broadly similar to onshore
  investment bonds but are issued by life insurance companies based
  outside the UK (in jurisdictions like the Isle of Man, Dublin,
  Luxembourg, etc.). They too offer tax-deferred accumulation of
  investment income and gains, but because they are based offshore,
  **the investment growth inside the policy is effectively untaxed** (no
  UK corporation tax is applied within the fund). This is often called
  "gross roll-up." The result is that the bond can potentially grow
  faster (no internal tax drag), but the trade-off comes at the point of
  taxation for the investor.

- **Income Tax Treatment of Gains:** Like onshore bonds, any profit
  (gain) on an offshore bond is subject to Income Tax as a chargeable
  event gain when it arises (e.g. on surrender or certain withdrawals).
  **However, offshore bond gains have no basic rate tax credit** --
  unlike a UK bond, the insurer hasn't paid UK tax, so the full gain is
  taxable in the hands of the
  policyholder[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/gains-on-foreign-life-insurance-policies-hs321-self-assessment-helpsheet/hs321-gains-on-foreign-life-insurance-policies-2025#:~:text=In%20these%20notes%20%E2%80%98gains%E2%80%99%20are,repayable%20basic%20rate%20tax%20credit).
  This means even a basic-rate taxpayer will have a liability on an
  offshore bond gain (whereas a basic-rate taxpayer would have no
  further tax on a UK bond gain). Offshore bond gains are taxed at the
  individual's marginal rate in the year of the chargeable event. If the
  gain pushes a basic-rate taxpayer into higher rate, the portion in the
  higher band will be at 40%, etc. The gain is treated as *savings
  income*, so in theory one could use any available starting-rate band
  or Personal Savings Allowance against it, but typically offshore bond
  gains are taken by higher-net-worth investors who may fully use those
  allowances elsewhere.

- **5% Withdrawals & Deferral:** Offshore bonds also offer the **5% per
  year tax-deferred withdrawal** feature, identical to onshore bonds,
  allowing up to 5% of the invested amount to be taken annually without
  immediate tax (cumulatively up to 100% of the original
  premium)[[taxscape.deloitte.com]{.underline}](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/insurance-bonds.aspx#:~:text=Withdrawals).
  Any excess withdrawal over the available 5% allowance triggers a
  chargeable gain at that time. The ability to **segment** the bond into
  multiple policies and assign segments to others (e.g. to adult
  children) without a tax charge is another feature often used, just as
  with onshore policies (assignments made as a gift are not chargeable
  events).

- **Top-Slicing Relief:** Offshore bond gains also qualify for
  top-slicing relief to mitigate large one-off gains. The top-slice
  calculation for offshore bonds will average the gain over the years
  held, potentially reducing the effective tax rate on the
  gain[[taxscape.deloitte.com]{.underline}](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/insurance-bonds.aspx#:~:text=Taxation%20of%20chargeable%20event%20gains).
  This can be especially useful if the gain would otherwise push the
  investor into a much higher tax bracket in the year of encashment.

- **No CGT, All Income Basis:** As with onshore bonds, offshore bond
  gains are not subject to Capital Gains Tax. All the growth is
  eventually assessed to Income Tax. There is no benefit of a CGT annual
  exempt amount, but conversely using an offshore bond can avoid
  incurring annual taxable dividends or interest that might arise if one
  held the assets directly -- instead, those are deferred within the
  bond.

- **Interest & Dividends Inside the Bond:** Inside an offshore bond, the
  investments grow gross (without UK tax). Interest, rental income, and
  dividends received by the bond's underlying investments are typically
  not taxed (many offshore jurisdictions impose no tax on investment
  income; and withholding taxes may be minimized). The investor receives
  no direct income; all such returns accumulate within the bond until
  withdrawn.

- **Tax on Death:** The death of the life assured (or policyholder, as
  relevant) on an offshore bond triggers a chargeable event just as with
  an onshore bond. The gain up to the date of death would be calculated
  and is taxable as income on the deceased's final return or the policy
  beneficiary, as
  appropriate[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/gains-on-uk-life-insurance-policies-hs320-self-assessment-helpsheet/hs320-gains-on-uk-life-insurance-policies-2025#:~:text=8).
  There is no forgiveness of the income tax due; death is simply another
  chargeable event. For Inheritance Tax, the **offshore bond's value**
  at death is part of the estate (if the policy was owned outright by
  the deceased). Offshore bonds are often used in conjunction with
  trusts -- placing the bond in a trust can exclude it from the estate
  for IHT (assuming it was a genuine gift into trust and the individual
  survives 7 years, etc.). The bond's tax-deferred nature is unaffected
  by being in trust, aside from who is assessable on gains.

- **Additional Relief -- Time Apportionment:** One unique relief with
  offshore policies is **Time Apportionment Relief**. If the
  policyholder has spent time as a non-UK resident during the ownership
  of the bond, the chargeable gain can be reduced proportionally for the
  non-resident period. Essentially, the gain is reduced in proportion to
  the time the individual was outside the UK. This relief recognizes
  that gains accruing while not UK-resident shouldn't be fully subject
  to UK tax. (This can significantly reduce the tax on an offshore bond
  gain if, for example, an investor lived abroad for part of the bond's
  term.)

- **Relevant Allowances:** As noted, offshore bond gains are savings
  income. Investors with little or no other income could potentially use
  the £5,000 0% starting rate band for savings and the Personal Savings
  Allowance against an offshore bond gain. In practice, many who use
  offshore bonds are higher-rate taxpayers (seeking deferral), so those
  allowances might not apply. There are no annual investment limits on
  offshore bonds -- one can invest any amount (this contrasts with ISAs
  or pensions which have annual caps). This makes offshore bonds a
  flexible vehicle for large sums, with the trade-off being the eventual
  income tax on gains.

- **Summary of Use:** Offshore bonds do not provide a tax *exemption*,
  but rather tax *deferral* and potential arbitrage (you might arrange
  to realize gains in a year you are abroad or have low income, etc.).
  The entire gain is eventually taxable, but the timing is in the
  investor's control. They are often used by those who have exhausted
  other tax shelters (like ISAs/pensions) and want investments to grow
  without annual UK tax or who have estate planning objectives (using
  trusts or assigning parts of the bond to lower-taxed family members).
  Always, the distinction is: **Onshore bond -- slower growth
  (internally taxed) but with a 20% credit on gains; Offshore bond --
  gross growth but full-rate tax on gains**.

**Venture Capital Trusts (VCTs)**

- **Overview:** A Venture Capital Trust is a HMRC-approved investment
  fund (a listed company) that pools investors' money to invest in
  small, high-risk unquoted trading companies. VCTs are intended to
  encourage investment in smaller enterprises by offering significant
  tax incentives. The reliefs apply to **newly issued VCT shares** (i.e.
  when you subscribe to a VCT's fundraising; buying existing VCT shares
  on the market does not give income tax relief, though dividends and
  gains remain tax-free if the shares are qualifying VCT shares).

- **Income Tax Relief:** Investors subscribing to new VCT shares can
  claim **30% Income Tax relief** on the amount invested, up to a
  maximum investment of **£200,000 per tax
  year**[[taxscape.deloitte.com]{.underline}](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/venture-capital-trusts.aspx#:~:text=,in%20the%20previous%20tax%20year).
  This means up to £60,000 reduction in income tax liability per year
  (30% of £200k) if sufficient tax is owed. The relief is given as a tax
  credit (it reduces your tax bill, but cannot exceed the tax you
  actually owe -- it won't create a refund beyond tax paid). To retain
  this upfront relief, you **must hold the VCT shares for at least 5
  years**. If you sell or otherwise dispose of the shares within five
  years, the 30% income tax relief on those shares is clawed back by
  HMRC[[octopusinvestments.com]{.underline}](https://octopusinvestments.com/resources/guides/how-to-claim-vct-tax-relief/#:~:text=,any%20upfront%20tax%20relief%20claimed).
  (An exception is made if you die -- death does *not* trigger a
  clawback of VCT income tax relief, regardless of the holding
  period[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/venture-capital-schemes-manual/vcm51090#:~:text=Death).)
  There is no carry-back of VCT relief to a previous year -- the relief
  applies only in the year of
  investment[[taxscape.deloitte.com]{.underline}](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/venture-capital-trusts.aspx#:~:text=,in%20the%20previous%20tax%20year).

- **Dividend Income:** **Dividends from VCT shares are completely
  tax-free** to
  investors[[taxscape.deloitte.com]{.underline}](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/venture-capital-trusts.aspx#:~:text=,are%20exempt%20from%20income%20tax).
  This applies both to shares you subscribed for and even to VCT shares
  purchased second-hand (the tax-free dividend perk applies to any
  qualifying VCT shares as long as the company maintains its VCT
  status)[[taxscape.deloitte.com]{.underline}](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/venture-capital-trusts.aspx#:~:text=,are%20exempt%20from%20income%20tax).
  There is no upper limit on the amount of VCT dividends that are
  tax-free, and VCT dividends do not use up your normal dividend
  allowance -- they are simply exempt. This makes VCTs particularly
  attractive to income-seeking investors in higher tax brackets, as the
  often-regular dividends (some VCTs pay periodic tax-free dividends
  from their investment exits) come with no further tax. *(If the VCT
  loses its approved status, dividends paid thereafter would become
  taxable as normal, but this is rare and the VCT must meet ongoing
  conditions to keep
  status[[taxscape.deloitte.com]{.underline}](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/venture-capital-trusts.aspx#:~:text=For%20income%20tax%20relief%20to,claimed%20may%20be%20clawed%20back).)*

- **Capital Gains Tax:** **No Capital Gains Tax is charged on profits
  from selling VCT shares**, provided the shares were qualifying VCT
  shares (and not withdrawn from the
  scheme)[[taxscape.deloitte.com]{.underline}](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/venture-capital-trusts.aspx#:~:text=exempt%20from%20income%20tax).
  In other words, any gain on disposal of VCT shares is **CGT-free** --
  you do not even need to report it on a tax return. Likewise, any
  capital losses on VCT shares are **not allowable** for CGT
  purposes[[taxscape.deloitte.com]{.underline}](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/venture-capital-trusts.aspx#:~:text=exempt%20from%20income%20tax)
  (the logic being, you can't claim a tax-deductible loss on an asset
  that could produce a tax-free gain). Note that unlike EIS/SEIS
  (below), VCTs do **not** offer any capital gains *deferral/exemption
  for other assets' gains* -- investing in a VCT will not defer an
  unrelated capital gain (VCT reliefs only relate to the income tax
  relief on the subscription, tax-free VCT dividends, and CGT freedom on
  the VCT shares
  themselves)[[taxscape.deloitte.com]{.underline}](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/venture-capital-trusts.aspx#:~:text=applies%20both%20to%20newly%20issued,acquired%20through%20a%20stock%20exchange).

- **Inheritance Tax:** VCT shares **do not qualify for Business Property
  Relief (BPR)** in the way that direct shares in small trading
  companies or EIS/SEIS shares
  might[[taxscape.deloitte.com]{.underline}](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/venture-capital-trusts.aspx#:~:text=gains%20made%20on%20disposal%20of,other%20assets).
  VCTs are themselves listed companies on the stock market (typically
  the London Stock Exchange main market), so HMRC views them as
  investment funds rather than direct business holdings. Therefore,
  **VCT shares are fully subject to Inheritance Tax** if still held at
  death (they form part of your estate value, with no special relief).
  The upside is that, as noted, if you die holding VCT shares before 5
  years are up, your estate/beneficiaries do **not** have to repay the
  30% income tax relief -- death is not treated as a disposal for the
  relief clawback
  condition[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/venture-capital-schemes-manual/vcm51090#:~:text=Death).

- **Tax-Deferred/Sheltering Features:** Apart from the main reliefs (30%
  upfront reduction in income tax, tax-free dividends, no CGT on exit),
  VCTs don't have a deferral mechanism for external gains like EIS does.
  The incentive is more about tax-free income and gains going forward.
  One could view the tax-free dividend as a form of sheltering ongoing
  income from tax. Also, the fact that gains on the VCT itself are never
  taxed is a strong sheltering feature for growth. There is no annual
  limit on tax-free dividends or gains -- the only limit is on how much
  new investment qualifies for the upfront 30% relief (£200k/year).

- **Personal Allowances/Reliefs:** The £200,000 per year investment cap
  is the key limit -- beyond that, no income tax relief is available
  (though one could still invest more, it just wouldn't garner relief or
  additional tax protection beyond normal). The 30% relief cannot reduce
  your tax below zero -- you need to have at least as much Income Tax
  liability as the relief to benefit (any unused cannot be refunded or
  carried forward). VCT dividend income, being tax-free, does not use up
  your dividend allowance. If you have other dividends from outside a
  VCT, you still get the normal dividend allowance for those.
  **Example:** If you invested £50,000 in a VCT, you could reduce your
  Income Tax by £15,000 (30%) for that year, provided you owe at least
  £15k. If that VCT later pays, say, a 5% dividend (£2,500), that £2,500
  is tax-free on top of any other dividends you
  earn[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/venture-capital-schemes-tax-relief-for-investors#:~:text=You%20cannot%20carry%20forward%20unused,relief%20to%20future%20tax%20years).

- **Conditions and Timeline:** To keep the reliefs, the VCT itself and
  your ownership must meet conditions. You must not sell the shares
  within 5 years or the income tax relief is clawed
  back[[octopusinvestments.com]{.underline}](https://octopusinvestments.com/resources/guides/how-to-claim-vct-tax-relief/#:~:text=,any%20upfront%20tax%20relief%20claimed).
  Also, if you buy and sell VCT shares in the same VCT within six months
  (bed and breakfasting), relief can be denied. The VCT must also remain
  qualifying (investing at least 80% in unquoted trading companies,
  etc.). For dividends and CGT exemption, there is no time limit -- even
  after 5 years, as long as the VCT maintains its status, any dividends
  remain tax-free and any sale is CGT-free for you.

- **Legacy Note:** VCT relief percentages have been steady at 30% for
  many years (originally 20% in the mid-1990s, raised to 40% then
  settled at 30%). The £200k limit has also been longstanding. The
  government has announced the continuation of VCT (and EIS) schemes
  beyond 2025 (removing a previous sunset clause), so these remain
  available.

*(VCTs are higher-risk; while not a tax point, it's worth noting that
VCT shares can be illiquid and volatile, and the generous tax breaks are
to compensate for that risk.)*

**Enterprise Investment Scheme (EIS)**

- **Overview:** The Enterprise Investment Scheme is designed to
  encourage investment in small, early-stage **unquoted trading
  companies** by offering several tax reliefs. When you invest in
  qualifying EIS shares (newly issued shares of an eligible company),
  you can get income tax relief, capital gains benefits, and loss
  relief, as well as longer-term inheritance tax benefits due to
  business property relief.

- **Income Tax Relief:** An individual can invest up to **£1,000,000 per
  tax year** in EIS companies (or up to **£2,000,000** if at least the
  additional £1m is invested in "knowledge-intensive" companies, which
  are certain R&D-heavy startups). You receive **30% Income Tax relief**
  on the amount
  invested[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/venture-capital-schemes-tax-relief-for-investors#:~:text=Scheme%20Maximum%20annual%20investment%20you,Yes).
  For example, a £100,000 EIS investment yields £30,000 of income tax
  relief, which can be claimed either in the tax year of the investment
  or carried back to the previous tax year (EIS allows you to treat some
  or all of the investment as if made in the prior year, up to that
  year's
  limit)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/venture-capital-schemes-tax-relief-for-investors#:~:text=When%20you%20can%20claim%20Income,Tax%20relief).
  The relief is given as a reduction in your Income Tax bill
  (non-refundable beyond tax owed). To keep the relief, you must hold
  the shares for **at least 3 years** from the date of issue (and the
  company must continue to meet EIS qualifying conditions during that
  period). If you sell the shares or the company ceases to qualify
  within 3 years, the income tax relief is withdrawn/clawed back. (If
  you die within the 3 years, however, the relief is **not** clawed back
  -- death is not treated as a disposal or disqualifying
  event[[eisa.org.uk]{.underline}](https://eisa.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/EIS-Example-Inheritance-Tax-Relief-2021.pdf#:~:text=Over%20the%20same%20period%2C%20the,the%20deceased%20in%20this%20example).)
  You also can't be "connected" to the company (as an employee or owning
  \>30% stake, etc.) during the period from 2 years before to 3 years
  after investment, or relief can be denied.

- **Dividend Income:** **No special dividend tax exemption** applies to
  EIS investments. If the EIS company pays dividends, they are taxable
  to you as normal dividends (with the usual dividend allowance and
  rates). In reality, most EIS-stage companies are focused on growth and
  typically **do not pay dividends** in the early years (if at all) --
  investors expect returns via growth and eventual sale, not income.
  Thus, the lack of tax-free dividends is usually not a factor. (By
  contrast, VCTs pay tax-free dividends, but EIS companies usually
  reinvest profits rather than distribute them.)

- **Capital Gains Tax Relief -- Disposal:** Perhaps the most attractive
  part of EIS: if you hold the EIS shares for at least 3 years and have
  claimed the income tax relief, then **any gain on the sale of the
  shares is completely exempt from Capital Gains
  Tax**[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/venture-capital-schemes-tax-relief-for-investors#:~:text=Gains%20Tax%20when%20you%20sell,applicable%20Not%20applicable%20Yes%20No).
  You pay **0% CGT** on profits from a successful EIS investment,
  provided the conditions are met (and any initial income tax relief
  wasn't withdrawn). This CGT exemption applies no matter how large the
  gain, and it does not use up your annual CGT allowance -- it's a full
  exemption. If the shares are disposed of after the qualifying period,
  you simply ignore the gain for tax purposes (conversely, if you sell
  at a gain **before** 3 years or the company ceases qualifying, the
  gain would be subject to CGT or income tax and the initial relief
  lost).

- **Capital Gains Deferral Relief (Reinvestment Relief):** EIS also
  offers the ability to **defer tax on other capital gains**. If you
  have made a capital gain (e.g. from selling a property, shares, etc.),
  and you invest the proceeds (or part of them) into EIS qualifying
  shares, you can claim **EIS deferral relief** on that gain. This means
  the CGT on the original gain is deferred -- it doesn't need to be paid
  until the EIS shares are disposed of (or they cease to qualify, or you
  become
  non-resident)[[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/venture-capital-schemes-tax-relief-for-investors#:~:text=Defer%20when%20you%20pay%20Capital,deferral%20relief)[gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/venture-capital-schemes-tax-relief-for-investors#:~:text=You%E2%80%99ll%20need%20to%20pay%20the,tax%20when)]{.underline}.
  Key points:

  - There is **no limit** on the amount of gain you can defer (the £1m
    limit is for income tax relief, but you could defer, say, a £5m
    capital gain if you invest that much into EIS companies, though only
    £1m (or £2m for KIC) of the investment gets income relief).

  - The investment for deferral can be made 1 year before or up to 3
    years after the gain's disposal
    date[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/venture-capital-schemes-tax-relief-for-investors#:~:text=You%20must%20make%20the%20investment,after%20you%20sell%20the%20asset).
    So you have a roughly 4-year window to reinvest a gain into EIS for
    deferral.

  - The deferred gain will typically become taxable when you eventually
    dispose of the EIS shares (unless you once again reinvest in EIS or
    certain other events occur). If you die holding the EIS shares,
    **the deferred gain is wiped out entirely** -- no CGT is due at all
    (death resets the cost basis to market value for that asset and any
    deferred CGT does not
    revive)[[eisa.org.uk]{.underline}](https://eisa.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/EIS-Example-Inheritance-Tax-Relief-2021.pdf#:~:text=Over%20the%20same%20period%2C%20the,the%20deceased%20in%20this%20example).
    This is a significant benefit: EIS can be used as a tool to not just
    defer gains but potentially **avoid** CGT altogether if held until
    death.

  - Note: from April 2023, the government allowed deferral only for
    investments up to 5 April 2025 (the EIS 'sunset' was extended in the
    2023 Autumn Statement, so deferral continues beyond that; this is
    being updated, but the scheme will continue). For completeness,
    **Social Investment Tax Relief (SITR)**, a similar scheme for social
    enterprises, allowed deferral up to April 2023; but SITR is now
    closed to new investments.

- **Loss Relief:** EIS investments are high risk, and the tax system
  provides some cushion if things go badly. If you dispose of EIS shares
  at a **loss**, you can elect to **offset that loss against your Income
  Tax** (or against capital gains) -- effectively giving loss relief at
  your marginal tax rate. The loss for this purpose is calculated net of
  the income tax relief you originally received. For example, suppose
  you invest £100k in an EIS, get £30k income tax relief, and later sell
  the shares for £20k (an £80k loss). Net of the £30k relief, your
  effective economic loss is £50k. You can claim loss relief on that
  £50k against your income of the year of disposal or the previous year.
  If you're a 45% taxpayer, that's £22.5k back. Combined with the
  initial £30k relief, the total tax offsets would be £52.5k on a £100k
  investment loss, meaning your net loss is £47.5k. Alternatively, you
  could claim the loss as a capital loss in the normal way to offset
  other gains (but income offset is usually more valuable if you have
  the income).

- **Inheritance Tax:** EIS shares are unquoted trading company shares,
  and provided the company qualifies, **they should be eligible for 100%
  Business Property Relief** after **2 years of holding** (and still
  held at death). That means if you die holding the EIS shares, their
  value can be **excluded from your estate for IHT** (no 40% tax on
  them)[[taxscape.deloitte.com]{.underline}](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/venture-capital-trusts.aspx#:~:text=gains%20made%20on%20disposal%20of,other%20assets).
  This, combined with the fact that any deferred gains are forgiven on
  death[[eisa.org.uk]{.underline}](https://eisa.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/EIS-Example-Inheritance-Tax-Relief-2021.pdf#:~:text=Over%20the%20same%20period%2C%20the,the%20deceased%20in%20this%20example)
  and no CGT on growth, makes EIS-invested capital very tax-efficient in
  an estate planning context. In short, EIS shares held \>2 years can
  pass to beneficiaries free of **Inheritance Tax, Capital Gains Tax,
  and also free of any income tax liabilities** (since death isn't a
  clawback event and they likely didn't pay dividends). Note: If the
  shares are sold before death (after 3-year minimum), they become
  normal cash/proceeds in your estate (which would be taxable for IHT if
  you still have them at death, unless reinvested into other BPR assets
  or given away).

- **Other Allowances:** EIS initial income tax relief can be "carried
  back" to the previous tax year if you haven't used that year's EIS
  limit[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/venture-capital-schemes-tax-relief-for-investors#:~:text=When%20you%20can%20claim%20Income,Tax%20relief).
  This offers flexibility in claiming relief. The **annual investment
  cap** of £1m (or £2m for knowledge-intensive companies) is per person
  -- a married couple could each invest up to those limits. EIS
  investments must be reported to HMRC via an EIS3 certificate process
  and claimed on your tax return. There's no direct "allowance" like an
  ISA allowance, but rather these limits on how much can qualify for
  relief. There is also no tax on any dividends received by an EIS fund
  (if any are paid) beyond normal rules -- but as mentioned, dividends
  are uncommon.

- **Tax Example:** Suppose you invest £50,000 in an EIS this year. You
  can reduce your Income Tax by £15,000 (30%). After 4 years, the
  company is sold and your shares are worth £120,000. You pay **zero
  CGT** on the £70k gain -- it's entirely tax-free. If instead the
  company failed and the shares became worthless, you could claim an
  income tax loss on the £35k net cost (since you got £15k relief
  initially, your net cost was £35k), saving e.g. £14k if you're a 40%
  taxpayer. Additionally, if you had £50k of gains from a prior asset
  sale, you could invest that in the EIS and defer the CGT on that £50k
  gain; if you still held the EIS at death, that deferred CGT liability
  would disappear entirely.

*(EIS is subject to various rules regarding the company size, use of
funds, risk to capital requirement, etc., but those are beyond the scope
of this tax-focused summary. The EIS scheme has been extended beyond
2025, ensuring these tax benefits continue to be available.)*

**Seed Enterprise Investment Scheme (SEIS)**

- **Overview:** SEIS is like a "younger sibling" of EIS, aimed at **very
  early-stage companies** (seed stage). It offers even more generous tax
  reliefs to compensate for the extremely high risk of investing in new
  startups. Many tax treatments mirror EIS, with some differences in
  limits and percentages.

- **Income Tax Relief:** You can invest up to **£200,000 per tax year**
  in SEIS-qualifying companies (limit increased in April 2023 from the
  previous £100k). You receive **50% Income Tax relief** on the amount
  invested[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/venture-capital-schemes-tax-relief-for-investors#:~:text=Scheme%20Maximum%20annual%20investment%20you,Yes).
  This is a huge incentive: for example, a £50,000 SEIS investment
  yields £25,000 off your tax bill. The relief works like EIS -- it
  reduces your Income Tax liability (non-refundable beyond what you owe)
  and can be claimed in the year of investment or carried back to the
  prior year (up to that year's SEIS limit). The shares must be held for
  **at least 3 years** to keep the relief, and the investor/company must
  meet similar conditions to EIS (no connection, etc.). Disposing of
  SEIS shares within 3 years or the company ceasing to qualify will lead
  to relief being withdrawn. The £200k limit is separate from the EIS
  limit -- one could do both EIS and SEIS in the same year (in practice,
  £200k SEIS + £1m EIS if one wished, given enough tax liability to
  cover the relief).

- **Dividend Income:** As with EIS, **no special dividend exemption** --
  any dividends from SEIS companies are taxable as normal. However, seed
  companies almost never pay dividends in their first years (they
  usually reinvest any profits). So this is rarely relevant.

- **Capital Gains Tax Relief -- Disposal:** Like EIS, **SEIS shares are
  exempt from CGT on disposal** if held for ≥3 years and the initial
  income tax relief was claimed and not
  withdrawn[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/venture-capital-schemes-tax-relief-for-investors#:~:text=Enterprise%20Investment%20Scheme%20Yes%20on,applicable%20Not%20applicable%20Yes%20No).
  Any gain on selling SEIS shares is **tax-free**. Given the high-risk
  nature, the hope is a few investments might multiply in value -- those
  successful outcomes would be completely tax-exempt gains.

- **50% Reinvestment Relief for Capital Gains into SEIS:** SEIS
  originally introduced a unique CGT incentive: if you reinvest a
  capital gain into SEIS shares, **50% of that gain is exempt from
  Capital Gains Tax**. This is slightly different from EIS deferral:

  - With SEIS, it's not just a deferral, it's an actual exemption of
    half the gain permanently. The other half of the gain is taxed as
    usual in the year of the original disposal.

  - There is a cap: you can get this 50% CGT exemption on reinvested
    gains up to the SEIS investment limit. Currently, that means you can
    exempt up to **£100,000** of gains per year (which is 50% of the max
    £200k SEIS
    investment)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/venture-capital-schemes-tax-relief-for-investors#:~:text=You%20can%20get%20Capital%20Gains,you%20can%20get%20is%20%C2%A3100%2C000).
    In practical terms, if you had a £100k capital gain from selling
    shares, and you invest £100k into SEIS, £50k of that gain would be
    CGT-free, and you'd only pay CGT on the other £50k. If you invested
    £200k into SEIS and had at least £200k of gains, you'd exempt £100k
    of those gains from
    CGT[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/venture-capital-schemes-tax-relief-for-investors#:~:text=You%20can%20get%20Capital%20Gains,you%20can%20get%20is%20%C2%A3100%2C000).

  - This SEIS reinvestment relief applies to gains realized in the same
    tax year as the SEIS investment (or you can carry back to prior year
    gains if you carry back the SEIS income tax relief as well).
    Originally this was a temporary relief but it has been made
    permanent and expanded with the new limits.

  - Note: The *deferred* half of the gain (the other 50%) does not get
    deferred like EIS; it's just taxed normally. Only half is relieved.
    So SEIS doesn't defer the remaining half -- it's simply a straight
    exemption on half the gain.

- **Loss Relief:** SEIS, like EIS, qualifies for **loss relief**. If the
  company fails and you lose your money, you can offset the loss against
  income or gains. The loss is calculated after taking into account the
  50% income tax relief. For example, invest £20k in SEIS, get £10k
  relief. If the investment becomes worthless, you have a £10k effective
  loss. You could reduce your taxable income by £10k (saving, say, £4k
  if you're a 40% taxpayer). Combined with the initial £10k relief, that
  means out of your £20k, you recoup £14k via tax reliefs -- limiting
  your downside to £6k (while your upside on a winner could be entirely
  tax-free). This high leverage is what makes SEIS attractive to those
  with appetite for startup investing.

- **Inheritance Tax:** SEIS shares, being shares in small unquoted
  companies, are generally eligible for **100% Business Property Relief
  after 2 years**, just like EIS. Therefore, if you hold SEIS shares
  until death (and they've been held \>2 years), their value can be
  **exempt from Inheritance
  Tax**[[taxscape.deloitte.com]{.underline}](https://taxscape.deloitte.com/article/venture-capital-trusts.aspx#:~:text=gains%20made%20on%20disposal%20of,other%20assets).
  SEIS is particularly potent in estate planning: funds invested in
  SEIS, if survived 2 years, leave the estate IHT-free (and meanwhile,
  had the income tax and CGT benefits too). Of course, one must weigh
  this against the very real risk that the company might not survive or
  might lose value in that time.

- **On Death/Clawback:** If you die holding SEIS shares before the
  3-year minimum, similar to EIS, the income tax relief is **not clawed
  back** and any CGT reinvestment relief is not
  lost[[eisa.org.uk]{.underline}](https://eisa.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/EIS-Example-Inheritance-Tax-Relief-2021.pdf#:~:text=Over%20the%20same%20period%2C%20the,the%20deceased%20in%20this%20example).
  Death is not considered a disposal that voids the relief (the law for
  SEIS mirrors EIS in this regard). Thus, your estate would not have to
  repay the 50% initial tax relief even if death occurred within 3
  years.

- **Allowances/Limits:** The **£200,000/year SEIS cap** is the big limit
  (doubled from £100k as of April
  2023)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/venture-capital-schemes-tax-relief-for-investors#:~:text=Scheme%20Maximum%20annual%20investment%20you,Yes).
  This is separate from the EIS limit. The 50% CGT reinvestment
  exemption is effectively limited by the SEIS investment limit as
  explained (max £100k gain exempt per
  year)[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/venture-capital-schemes-tax-relief-for-investors#:~:text=You%20can%20get%20Capital%20Gains,you%20can%20get%20is%20%C2%A3100%2C000).
  SEIS also has tighter rules on company size (e.g. company must have
  \<£350k in assets and \<25 employees at time of investment, etc.)
  which investors need not remember except that it means opportunities
  are limited and often fill up fast.

- **Example:** You realize a £50,000 capital gain on a property sale.
  You invest £50,000 into a SEIS that year. You get £25k off your income
  tax. Additionally, £25k of your property gain (50% of £50k) is exempt
  from CGT -- if that £50k gain would have been taxed at 20%, you save
  another £5k. So immediately, £30k of tax is saved, on a £50k
  investment. Now suppose in 5 years that SEIS investment either goes to
  zero or multiplies. If it goes to zero, your net loss was only £20k
  (because of £30k tax saved), and you can even get loss relief on the
  net £25k cost to further reduce that loss. If it multiplies, say to
  £200k, you pay **no CGT** on the £150k gain. Clearly, SEIS is very
  tax-favored to balance the extreme risk.

- **Note:** SEIS was made more generous in 2023 (limits raised),
  signaling continued support. It's a niche but powerful tool for those
  willing to invest in startups. Keep in mind you cannot both claim EIS
  and SEIS on the same investment -- they are separate schemes for
  companies at different stages.

**National Savings & Investments (NS&I) Products**

NS&I is a government-backed savings institution offering various
products. Tax treatment varies: some NS&I products are **tax-free**,
while others are taxable just like bank accounts. Below we cover both
current and legacy NS&I products and their tax positions:

- **Premium Bonds:** Premium Bonds are a popular NS&I product. Instead
  of interest, they offer monthly prize draws. **All prizes from Premium
  Bonds are tax-free** -- you do not pay any Income Tax or CGT on
  winnings[[nsandi.com]{.underline}](https://www.nsandi.com/tax-free-saving#:~:text=%2A%20Tax).
  This effectively makes Premium Bonds interest equivalent (the "prize
  rate") free of tax. For example, if you win £100 or £1,000 or even the
  £1 million jackpot, it's tax-free. Premium Bond prizes are not
  considered interest, so they don't count toward your Personal Savings
  Allowance either. Premium Bonds can be held up to £50,000 per person.
  While there's no direct interest to reinvest, "returns" via prizes are
  tax-exempt. For **capital gains**, there is no concept of gain on
  Premium Bonds -- you always can cash them in at par (your original
  stake), so CGT doesn't apply. On **death**, Premium Bonds become part
  of the estate (no special IHT relief). NS&I allows Premium Bonds to
  continue participating in prize draws for up to 12 months after the
  owner's death, with any prizes still paid tax-free to the
  estate/beneficiaries. In summary, Premium Bonds' key perk is
  **tax-free prize
  "income"**[[nsandi.com]{.underline}](https://www.nsandi.com/tax-free-saving#:~:text=Premium%20Bonds.%203.60,free%20prizes%20each%20month).

- **NS&I Direct ISA and Junior ISA:** NS&I offers a **Direct ISA** (for
  adults) and a **Junior ISA** for children. These follow the same rules
  as any **ISA** (discussed above) -- interest is **tax-free**, no CGT
  on growth,
  etc[[nsandi.com](https://www.nsandi.com/tax-free-saving#:~:text=Direct%20ISA)[nsandi.com](https://www.nsandi.com/tax-free-saving#:~:text=Image%3A%20Junior%20ISA)]{.underline}.
  The Direct ISA pays interest free of tax (currently \~3.5%
  variable)[[nsandi.com]{.underline}](https://www.nsandi.com/tax-free-saving#:~:text=Direct%20ISA).
  The Junior ISA similarly pays tax-free interest. These count toward
  the respective ISA subscription limits (£20k for adult ISA, £9k for
  JISA). There is nothing unique about NS&I's ISAs except the backing by
  the Treasury and perhaps the interest rate; tax treatment is standard
  ISA.

- **NS&I Index-Linked Savings Certificates (Historical Issue):** These
  are *legacy products* no longer on general sale to new investors (as
  of the last several years), but many people continue to hold past
  issues. They are unique in that they offer an interest rate linked to
  inflation (Retail Prices Index) plus a fixed bonus, over a set term
  (typically 3 or 5 years). **All returns (the inflation uplift and
  interest bonus) on Index-Linked Savings Certificates are completely
  tax-free**[[en.wikipedia.org]{.underline}](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index-linked_Savings_Certificates#:~:text=Index,payers.%20They%20are).
  That means no Income Tax on the interest and no CGT either -- NS&I
  explicitly defines "tax-free" for these as exempt from UK Income Tax
  and
  CGT[[nsandi.com]{.underline}](https://www.nsandi.com/files/asset/pdf/index-linked-savings-certificates-summary-n.pdf#:~:text=%5BPDF%5D%20NS%26I%20Index,the%20annual%20rate%20of).
  These certificates, therefore, provide an inflation-proof,
  **tax-sheltered** return. Similarly, **Fixed Interest Savings
  Certificates** (another legacy NS&I product) pay a fixed rate over a
  term, and the interest is also **tax-free**. While new sales have been
  stopped for some time, existing holders often have the option to
  rollover their certificates at maturity into new issues. From a tax
  perspective, these are as good as an ISA (actually even better in one
  sense: they don't use up an ISA allowance). On death, the accrued
  interest is still tax-free up to the date of death and paid out
  without tax. However, the value of the certificates does count in the
  estate for IHT (no IHT relief).

- **Children's Bonds (Discontinued):** NS&I **Children's Bonds** were
  5-year fixed-rate bonds for children, offered in past years (no new
  issues after 2017). They had a maximum investment of £3,000 per issue.
  Importantly, **interest on Children's Bonds was tax-free**, both for
  the child and the parent who opened the
  bond[[nsandi.com]{.underline}](https://www.nsandi.com/files/asset/pdf/childrens-bonds-key-features.pdf#:~:text=,for%20the%20child%20and%20the).
  So, like Junior ISAs, these provided a tax-sheltered way to save for a
  child. Existing Children's Bonds continue to earn the rate until
  maturity and all interest is exempt from Income Tax and
  CGT[[nsandi.com]{.underline}](https://www.nsandi.com/files/asset/pdf/childrens-bonds-key-features.pdf#:~:text=,for%20the%20child%20and%20the).
  Once matured, the proceeds can be taken or moved to a JISA or other
  account (the tax-free status does not carry over except within NS&I
  when they allowed moving into JISAs). As with other NS&I, no special
  IHT treatment -- if the child (now grown) passed before cashing
  (unlikely scenario), it would be part of their estate, though
  typically these bonds would belong to the child (and children have
  their own IHT threshold potentially if they passed as minors with an
  estate).

- **NS&I Income Bonds and Direct Saver:** These are taxable NS&I savings
  accounts. **Income Bonds** pay monthly interest (currently around 2-3%
  variable) and **Direct Saver** is an easy-access savings account. The
  interest from these accounts is **taxable**, just like interest from
  any bank account. NS&I does not deduct tax at source (since 2016 all
  savings interest is paid gross), so if you're a taxpayer you may need
  to declare the interest if it exceeds your Personal Savings Allowance.
  The **Personal Savings Allowance** (PSA) means that the first £1,000
  of interest per year is tax-free for basic-rate taxpayers (£500 for
  higher-rate, £0 for additional-rate). So many people won't actually
  pay tax on NS&I Income Bond interest if it falls under those limits.
  But importantly, NS&I Income Bonds/Direct Saver interest **is not
  tax-exempt by default** -- it simply benefits from the same PSA as any
  other savings. There's no NS&I-specific exemption here. These accounts
  are backed by HM Treasury (as are all NS&I products), and that is an
  advantage for security, but not for tax. **Capital Gains** are not
  relevant for cash accounts (no CGT on cash savings). On **death**, any
  accrued interest up to date of death is taxable as the deceased's
  income (though banks/NS&I often pay it gross to the estate, the estate
  may have to account for any tax if the deceased's PSA was used up).
  The account value goes into the estate for IHT like any cash.

- **NS&I Guaranteed Growth Bonds & Guaranteed Income Bonds (Issues for
  general public):** These have come and gone; notably a special **65+
  Guaranteed Growth Bond** was offered in 2015 with attractive rates
  (dubbed "Pensioner Bonds"). Currently, NS&I offers **Guaranteed Growth
  Bonds** (1-year or longer fixed term) and **Guaranteed Income Bonds**
  (which pay out interest periodically) to online customers. These pay a
  fixed interest rate. The interest is **taxable** (they are not ISA or
  otherwise tax-free). NS&I provides the interest gross. So these are
  analogous to fixed deposits with a bank, just government-backed. If
  you have a large amount in them, interest above your PSA will be
  taxable at your marginal rate. There is no special tax relief -- their
  benefit is the security and occasionally higher rates, not tax.

- **Green Savings Bonds:** A relatively new NS&I product (launched 2021)
  -- a 3-year fixed rate bond aimed at funding green projects. These too
  pay interest (fixed over 3 years) that is **taxable as savings
  interest**. They are not ISA-designated, so they don't have tax-free
  status. Essentially, treat them like any other savings bond for tax --
  the interest counts as income in the year it is paid (which for a
  3-year bond might be annually or at maturity; NS&I originally
  structured them to pay at maturity, but later issues pay annually).
  Use of your PSA could cover some or all of it. No CGT issues (no
  transfer of principal, just interest).

- **National Savings Certificates (Historic -- "Pensioners' Bonds",
  etc.):** Over the decades NS&I had various products such as
  "Pensioners' Guaranteed Income Bonds", "Capital Bonds", etc. Most
  followed one of two models: either taxable interest or tax-free
  interest. Generally:

  - If NS&I labels the product as "tax-free" (as they do with ISAs,
    Premium Bonds, Savings Certificates, Children's Bonds), then the
    interest/prizes are exempt from Income Tax and CGT by statute.

  - Otherwise, the interest is taxable under normal rules.

NS&I's website clearly marks current products as "tax-free" or
not[[nsandi.com](https://www.nsandi.com/tax-free-saving#:~:text=Tax)[nsandi.com](https://www.nsandi.com/tax-free-saving#:~:text=3.50%25%20tax)]{.underline}.
For example, as of now the only tax-free NS&I products on sale are
Premium Bonds and the ISAs. The rest (Direct/Junior Saver, Income Bonds,
Guaranteed Bonds, Green Bonds) are taxable. If you still hold older
issues of things like Index-linked Certificates, those remain tax-free.

- **Inheritance Tax:** None of the NS&I products in themselves carry
  inheritance tax exemptions. They are often just cash or cash-like
  investments. All NS&I holdings (except possibly some older War Bonds
  if any exist) will form part of your estate. One misconception some
  have: since Premium Bonds are government-backed, some wonder if they
  avoid IHT -- they do not; they are just as subject to IHT as cash
  (though obviously an executor may hold them for up to a year of prize
  draws, any prizes won in that period are still tax-free and belong to
  the estate). One could use **gifts or trusts** to remove NS&I holdings
  from an estate, but that's general estate planning, not a feature of
  NS&I.

- **Security and Other Notes:** While not tax-related, a major draw of
  NS&I (particularly for higher balances) is that *all* funds are 100%
  guaranteed by HM Treasury (not just £85k as with FSCS limits in
  banks)[[nsandi.com]{.underline}](https://www.nsandi.com/tax-free-saving#:~:text=1.%20,hands).
  This can influence behavior of investors with large sums, but doesn't
  change the taxability of interest earned.

- **Summary of NS&I Taxation:** To sum up -- **Tax-free NS&I products**:
  Premium Bonds (prizes tax-free), NS&I ISAs (by definition tax-free),
  Savings Certificates (if you have them, interest tax-free), Children's
  Bonds (interest tax-free). **Taxable NS&I products**: Direct Saver,
  Income Bonds, Guaranteed Growth/Income Bonds, Green Bonds, Investment
  Account -- all interest is taxable like any bank interest (with PSAs
  available). Always check the NS&I product details; if it says
  "tax-free", HMRC treats it as such by law; if not, assume it's
  taxable.

**Other Investment Products and Personal Tax Considerations**

*(The question specifically listed major tax-advantaged products, which
we have covered above. For completeness, here are a few additional notes
on other personal investment scenarios in the UK and how they're
taxed:)*

- **General (Taxable) Investment Accounts:** If you invest outside of
  any tax wrapper -- for example, buying shares, funds, or bonds in a
  regular brokerage account -- the returns are subject to normal
  taxation. **Dividends** from UK or foreign companies are taxable,
  though each individual has a **Dividend Allowance** (£1,000 for
  2023/24, dropping to £500 for 2024/25) which means that amount of
  dividend income per year is 0%
  taxed[[moneyhelper.org.uk]{.underline}](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/savings/investing/stocks-and-shares-isas#:~:text=Everyone%20gets%20a%20%C2%A3500%20tax,tax%20year%20before%20paying%20tax).
  Above that, dividends are taxed at 8.75% (basic rate), 33.75%
  (higher), 39.35% (additional) as of 2025. **Interest** from cash
  deposits or bond coupons is also taxable, with the **Personal Savings
  Allowance** (£1,000 for basic rate, £500 for higher rate, £0 for
  additional) available to shield some
  interest[[moneyhelper.org.uk]{.underline}](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/savings/investing/stocks-and-shares-isas#:~:text=Investments%20that%20pay%20interest%20,if%20held%20within%20an%20ISA).
  **Capital gains** in a general account are subject to CGT (10% basic
  rate, 20% higher rate for most assets; 18%/28% for residential
  property gains). There is an **Annual Exempt Amount** (capital gains
  allowance) each year (£6,000 in 2023/24, falling to £3,000 in 2024/25)
  -- only gains above this are taxed. Losses can be offset against
  gains. These "general" investments don't have the upfront or
  specialized reliefs that EIS/VCT/ISA do; thus investors often use the
  tax-advantaged products first.

- **Personal Pensions (e.g. SIPPs):** While not asked, note that
  pensions (Self-Invested Personal Pensions, etc.) are another
  tax-advantaged wrapper: contributions get Income Tax relief,
  investments grow tax-free inside, and 25% of the pot can be taken
  tax-free at withdrawal, etc. They also are usually outside the estate
  for IHT. Pensions are a broad topic on their own, but it's worth
  mentioning that beyond the above investment products, pensions are a
  key personal investment vehicle with significant tax advantages.

- **Social Investment Tax Relief (SITR):** Now closed to new investments
  (ended April 2023), SITR was akin to EIS for social enterprises
  (charitable trading enterprises) offering 30% income tax relief and
  capital gains deferral on investments up to
  £1m[[gov.uk]{.underline}](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/venture-capital-schemes-tax-relief-for-investors#:~:text=companies%2030,Yes).
  Existing SITR investments will still carry their reliefs for the
  prescribed period, but no new SITR after the sunset.

- **Lifetime ISAs (LISA) and Help-to-Buy ISAs:** These are ISA variants
  aimed at first home purchase and/or retirement. **Help-to-Buy ISA**
  (no new accounts after Nov 2019) offered a government bonus 25% on
  savings up to a limit for first home purchase. They functioned as cash
  ISAs (interest tax-free) with the bonus not taxable (it's a bonus, not
  income). **Lifetime ISA** (still available) allows £4,000/year
  contribution (counts toward the £20k ISA limit) with a 25% government
  bonus added (max £1k bonus a year). All LISA interest/gains are
  tax-free as in an ISA. The bonus is not taxed. If withdrawn for a
  first home (meeting conditions) or after age 60, no penalty; if
  withdrawn for other reasons, a 25% penalty applies (effectively
  clawing back the bonus and a bit more). For our context, the important
  note is LISAs are just as tax-free as any ISA regarding growth; the
  unique element is the government bonus (which doesn't have a "tax"
  treatment per se, it's simply added money).

- **Offshore Funds and Others:** If investing in certain non-reporting
  offshore funds or products, there can be special tax rules (e.g. gains
  taxed as income). These are niche and beyond scope, but one should be
  aware some investments can have unexpected tax (always check if an
  offshore fund has "reporting status" to get CGT treatment on gains).

Please note that tax rates and allowances are as of the 2025/26 tax
year. Tax rules can change, so it's always wise to consult the latest
HMRC guidance or seek professional advice for current figures.

Each of the products above comes with its own set of conditions and risk
profiles, and the tax benefits are one aspect to consider. The UK
government (via HMRC) provides detailed guidance on these schemes -- for
further reading, see official sources like HMRC's Venture Capital
Schemes Manual (for VCT/EIS/SEIS) and HMRC helpsheets (e.g. HS320/321
for life insurance bonds) and the **gov.uk** guidance pages for ISAs and
NS&I. The links and citations provided point to some of these official
resources for verification and more detail on specific points.
