## What this is, when it applies, who it is for

Every child under eighteen has their own Junior ISA allowance each tax year,
entirely separate from their parents' allowances. Money inside the wrapper
grows free of tax on interest, dividends, and capital gains until the child
turns eighteen, at which point the account becomes the child's own adult ISA.
A household saving for children — for education, a future deposit, or simply
a head start — can therefore shelter family money over and above everything
the parents wrap in their own names. The strategy applies to users with
dependent children under eighteen recorded in their household; the money
belongs to the child from the outset and passes into their control at
eighteen, which is the feature families most need to weigh.

## Why Fynla quantifies it this way

Fynla counts the user's recorded children under eighteen and quantifies the
household's total Junior ISA capacity — the per-child allowance multiplied
across the children, refreshed every tax year. It deliberately attaches no
tax-saved headline, because the saving depends on what the money would
otherwise earn and where it would otherwise sit; the dependable fact is the
capacity itself, sitting alongside the parents' own allowances rather than
inside them. Each child's allowance is checked from the household's recorded
dates of birth, so the capacity shrinks naturally as children reach eighteen.

## Where it sits in sequence

Junior ISA capacity never competes with the parents' own wrapper strategies —
the allowances are separate, so filling a child's Junior ISA costs the
parents none of their own ISA room. It has no ordering constraints or
conflicts in the catalogue. The natural companion is the junior pension,
which shelters money for the same children on a much longer lock: the Junior
ISA money arrives at eighteen, the pension decades later, and households
often want a deliberate mix of the two horizons.

## Claim tier and voicing

Judgement tier: the capacity is plain arithmetic, but committing money is a
family decision — the gift is irrevocable, and an eighteen-year-old controls
the proceeds outright, which not every household is comfortable with. Fyn
therefore hedges, presenting the Junior ISA as capacity the family may want
to consider using, and is always explicit that the money becomes the child's
own at eighteen.
