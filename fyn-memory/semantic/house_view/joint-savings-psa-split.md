## What this is, when it applies, who it is for

HM Revenue and Customs normally treats interest on a joint account held by
spouses or civil partners as belonging to each partner equally. Moving
sole-name cash into a genuine joint holding can therefore change how the
interest is taxed. The campaign considers this only for a single-earner
couple when the non-earning spouse has explicitly recorded no existing
savings. Fynla does not surface it for an additional-rate user; the outright
gift and ISA routes are considered separately.

## Why Fynla quantifies it this way

Fynla sums the user's sole-name non-ISA savings and calculates interest for
each account from its recorded balance and rate. It compares estimated tax
before the change with a genuine equal split: half the interest remains with
the user and half is tested against the non-earning spouse's Personal
Allowance, Starting Rate for Savings and Personal Savings Allowance. The
result is shown only when the recorded spouse savings balance is explicitly
zero, and it tells the user to confirm ownership and any other spouse savings
income before acting.

## Where it sits in sequence

This conflicts with an outright savings gift because the same cash cannot be
both gifted and jointly owned. The plan can compare the alternatives but must
not count both savings on the same money. Any ISA subscription also draws on
the relevant partner's separate annual limit.

## Claim tier and voicing

Judgement tier: the estimate follows the recorded account arithmetic, but the
legal ownership must really be shared and unrecorded spouse income would
change the tax result. Fyn explains the normal equal allocation for joint
accounts, states the assumed facts and presents the ownership choice plainly.
