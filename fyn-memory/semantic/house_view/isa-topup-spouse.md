## What this is, when it applies, who it is for

Every adult has their own ISA allowance each tax year, whether or not they
earn — and ISAs are strictly individual accounts, since joint ISAs do not
exist under UK law. In a single-earner couple that means the non-earning
spouse carries a full, separate allowance that often goes unused while the
household's savings crowd into the earner's name. Opening or topping up an
ISA in the spouse's own name shelters more of the household's money from tax
on interest, dividends, and gains. The strategy applies whenever the spouse's
recorded ISA position shows unused allowance for the current year; it is for
couples whose saving outruns one allowance, and it assumes the household is
comfortable with the money genuinely belonging to the spouse.

## Why Fynla quantifies it this way

Fynla estimates the spouse's headroom by setting their recorded existing ISA
balance against the annual allowance — a deliberately rough proxy, since
balances built up in earlier years also shrink the headroom shown, which
keeps the figure conservative rather than overstated. It deliberately
does not attach a tax-saved headline, because the saving depends on what the
money would otherwise earn and where it currently sits; the unused capacity
is the dependable fact, and the saving follows once specific cash or
investments are chosen to fill it. Where the cash to fill the allowance comes
from a gift between partners, the transfer itself is free of Capital Gains
Tax and Inheritance Tax.

## Where it sits in sequence

This is the single-earner counterpart of the ISA coordination strategy shown
to dual-earner couples. It has no ordering constraints or conflicts of its
own, and it works hand in hand with the savings-to-spouse gift: cash moved
into the spouse's name can be wrapped in the spouse's own ISA, turning a
one-off transfer into a permanent shelter. The spouse's allowance is separate
from the user's, so using it never crowds out the user's own ISA strategies.

## Claim tier and voicing

Mechanical tier: the headroom follows directly from the spouse's recorded
balance and the published limit, so Fyn states the position plainly —
how much room the spouse has and when it resets. Voicing is exact about
ownership: the account is the spouse's own individual ISA, the money in it
belongs to the spouse, and Fyn never phrases the move in a way that could
suggest a shared or joint ISA.
