## What this is, when it applies, who it is for

A married partner or civil partner who earns too little to use their whole
Personal Allowance can transfer a fixed slice of it to the other partner,
reducing the receiving partner's income tax bill every year the claim stays
in place. Eligibility is strict on the receiving side: the recipient must be
a basic-rate taxpayer, and the claim is not available when they pay the
higher or additional rate. That band test runs on total taxable income —
employment, dividends, and savings interest together — so a moderately paid
employee with a substantial dividend stream is a higher-rate taxpayer for
Marriage Allowance purposes and does not qualify, however ordinary their
salary looks. The strategy suits single-earner couples where one partner's
income sits below the Personal Allowance and the other pays the basic rate.

## Why Fynla quantifies it this way

Fynla checks eligibility from the household's recorded circumstances — the
marriage or civil partnership, the lower earner's unused allowance, and the
recipient's band judged on their total taxable income rather than salary
alone. The saving is the transferable slice of allowance multiplied by the
basic rate from the live tax configuration: the income tax the receiving
partner no longer pays on that slice. It is a modest but entirely
dependable amount, and it recurs every year the couple's circumstances stay
within the rules, which is why Fynla surfaces it whenever the gate is
genuinely passed and never when the recipient's band rules it out.

## Where it sits in sequence

Marriage Allowance stands alone in the composed plan: it costs nothing, asks
for no new money, and conflicts with no other strategy. The one
interaction worth knowing runs through the band test — income-shifting
strategies that move dividends or interest into the lower earner's name keep
the recipient's total income inside the basic band, which protects the claim,
whereas anything that pushes the recipient over the band boundary ends it.

## Claim tier and voicing

Mechanical tier: eligibility and the saving follow from recorded incomes and
published figures, so Fyn states the working directly — who transfers, who
receives, and what the recipient saves each year. Voicing is precise about
the direction of the transfer and the recipient-band rule, because the
commonest confusion is a higher-rate household assuming they qualify; where
the band rules it out, Fyn says so plainly rather than leaving the strategy
quietly absent.
